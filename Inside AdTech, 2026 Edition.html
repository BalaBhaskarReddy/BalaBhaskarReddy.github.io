      Preface to the 2026 edition

      For most of 2020–2023, books about advertising technology were written around a single expected event: Google Chrome switching off third-party cookies. Every chapter on identity, targeting and measurement was framed as preparation for that day. It never came. Google delayed the change three times, abandoned it in July 2024, and in October 2025 retired most of the Privacy Sandbox technologies it had built to replace cookies.

      Meanwhile the industry changed in other ways. Courts in the United States found Google liable for monopolising key parts of the ad tech stack, then chose to regulate rather than break it up. Spending concentrated in a handful of companies that own both the audience and the tools: Google, Meta and Amazon now take close to 60% of all ad spend outside China. Retail media became one of the largest channels in advertising. Apple, not Google, became the main constraint on cross-site identity, and in September 2026 began blocking named ad tech and identity companies at the network level in Safari. And AI moved from optimising bids to writing ads, running campaigns, negotiating deals through new agent protocols, and becoming an ad surface in its own right with ads in ChatGPT.

      This book explains ad tech from first principles and describes the industry as it actually stands in late 2026. Where something changed recently, the change is woven into the explanation rather than added as a footnote. Amber boxes highlight the most important 2022–2026 shifts; cobalt sidebars define terms. Interactive figures let you run an auction, pace a budget, attribute a conversion or audit a supply chain yourself.

      01

      Introduction

      Why ad tech is hard to understand, who this book is for, and how it is organised

      Every time a web page, app or streaming show loads an ad, a chain of software systems has decided, usually within a fraction of a second, which advertiser gets that moment, at what price, and how the result will be counted. That chain is advertising technology, or ad tech.

      Ad tech is notoriously difficult to learn. It is made of dozens of platform types with overlapping acronyms (DSP, SSP, DMP, CDP, MMP), each built to solve a problem created by the generation before it. Much of it operates out of sight: in HTTP redirects, JavaScript tags, server-to-server calls and auctions that complete before a page finishes rendering. The companies involved often describe their products in marketing language that blurs what the software actually does. And the rules keep changing, through browser updates, platform policies, privacy laws and court rulings.

      This book is written for anyone who needs a working mental model of the system: marketers who buy media, publishers who sell it, product managers and engineers who build ad tech or integrate with it, founders, investors, lawyers and policy people. It assumes no prior knowledge of advertising, but it does not shy away from technical detail where detail is what makes the system make sense.

      How the book is organised

      The chapters follow the life of an ad, from the business relationships that make advertising possible to the auctions that sell it and the controversies that surround it.

      • Foundations (chapters 2–5): the basic economics and vocabulary, the history of how today's platforms came to exist, the platforms themselves, and the formats and channels ads run in.
      • Mechanics (chapters 6–9): how ads are served, targeted and paced, how impressions, clicks and conversions are tracked and reported, and how media is bought, from manual insertion orders to real-time bidding, header bidding and agent-to-agent negotiation.
      • Data and identity (chapters 10–12): how users are recognised across sites and devices, how data is collected, combined and activated, and how results are attributed to the ads that caused them.
      • Trust and rules (chapters 13–14): ad fraud, waste, brand safety and viewability, then privacy law, browser and platform restrictions, and consent.
      • The business (chapters 15–16): how vendors and agencies make money, the build-versus-buy decision, and the state of the industry in 2026: antitrust outcomes, concentration, AI and what to watch next.
      The 2026 picture in one paragraph

      Four forces define ad tech today. Antitrust: Google lost both US cases and an EU case, but kept its businesses under behavioural remedies built around the open-source Prebid auction. Concentration: walled gardens and retail media networks take most new money. Apple: Safari and iOS, not Chrome, set the limits on cross-site identity. AI: it runs campaign types, generates creative, negotiates through agent protocols, and sells ads inside chat assistants.

      Key takeaways

      • Ad tech is the set of systems that decide, deliver, price and measure ads, mostly automatically and in milliseconds.
      • Each platform type exists to fix a problem left by an earlier one; learning the history makes the acronyms make sense.
      • The expected "cookieless" transition did not happen in Chrome; the industry was reshaped by courts, concentration, Apple and AI instead.
      02

      Advertising basics

      The advertiser–publisher bargain, the vocabulary of inventory and creatives, and how ads are priced

      Digital advertising rests on a simple exchange. Publishers attract an audience with content or services; advertisers pay to put messages in front of that audience. Everything else in this book is machinery for making that exchange faster, more targeted and more measurable.

      Advertisers and publishers

      An advertiser is any organisation paying to promote a product, service, cause or idea: a global consumer brand, a local plumber, a mobile game, a political campaign. Large advertisers usually work through agencies, which plan campaigns, buy media and report results on their behalf.

      A publisher is any owner of an audience that sells access to it: a news site, a recipe blog, a mobile game, a social network, a streaming service, a retailer's website, an airport screen network, and since 2026, AI assistants such as ChatGPT. The publisher's goal is to earn as much as possible from its audience without driving it away.

      Publishers have several ways to make money, and ads are only one of them:

      • Advertising: displaying ads alongside content, the dominant model for the open web, free apps and ad-supported streaming tiers.
      • Paywalls and subscriptions: charging readers directly, either a hard paywall or a metered model allowing a few free articles.
      • Registration walls: giving content in exchange for an account. Logins have become far more valuable since 2022 because an authenticated user is one of the few identity signals that works in every browser.
      • Commerce and services: selling products, affiliate links or events.
      • Data: licensing audience data to advertisers or data companies, a practice now heavily constrained by privacy law.

      Many publishers combine these. Netflix, which launched its ad-supported plan in November 2022, now earns from both subscriptions and ads; by May 2026 about 60% of its new sign-ups in ad markets chose the ad plan.

      The vocabulary of inventory

      InventoryThe total supply of ad opportunities a publisher can sell, usually counted in impressions over a period. "Premium" inventory is the most valuable: high on the page, on high-quality content, or reaching a hard-to-find audience.

      An ad slot (or placement) is the specific space on a page, screen or video stream where an ad can appear, defined by position and size. A web page might have a leaderboard at the top, a medium rectangle in the sidebar and a native unit inside the article. In video, slots are ad breaks: pre-roll before content, mid-roll during it, post-roll after. The collection of slots across a publisher's properties, multiplied by how many times they load, is its inventory.

      A creative is the ad itself: an image, HTML5 animation, video file, audio spot or block of text. Every placement has creative specifications: dimensions, file size, file type, duration, and rules about animation and sound. The IAB (Interactive Advertising Bureau) publishes standard sizes such as 300×250 (medium rectangle), 728×90 (leaderboard), 160×600 (wide skyscraper) and 320×50 (mobile banner), plus guidelines for building ads that are lightweight, load securely over HTTPS, and carry the AdChoices icon that links users to information about why they are seeing an ad.

      Measuring what happened

      An impression is counted each time an ad is served. Because a served ad may load below the fold or in a background tab, the industry also counts viewable impressions: by the Media Rating Council (MRC) standard, at least 50% of a display ad's pixels on screen for at least one continuous second, or 50% of a video for two continuous seconds (chapter 13 lets you test this yourself). A click is recorded when a user clicks the ad and is sent to a landing page, the page built to turn that visit into an action. A conversion is the action the advertiser wants: a purchase, sign-up, app install, lead form, or store visit.

      Campaign effectiveness is judged against the advertiser's goal. Brand campaigns care about reach, frequency, viewability, attention and brand lift. Performance campaigns care about click-through rate (CTR), conversion rate, cost per acquisition and return on ad spend (ROAS).

      Pricing models

      Media can be priced in several ways, and the model determines who carries the risk:

      • CPM (cost per mille): a price per thousand impressions. The publisher is paid for delivering the audience, whatever the outcome. CPM is the default in display, video, CTV and programmatic auctions. Variants include vCPM (per thousand viewable impressions).
      • CPC (cost per click): the advertiser pays only when someone clicks. The model of search advertising since Google moved AdWords from CPM to CPC in 2002, and in May 2026 the model OpenAI chose for self-serve ChatGPT ads.
      • CPA (cost per action/acquisition): the advertiser pays only when a defined conversion happens. The publisher or network carries the most risk; common in affiliate marketing and app-install campaigns (where it is called CPI, cost per install).
      • CPV and CPCV: cost per view or per completed view, used for video.
      • Flat fee or sponsorship: a fixed price for owning a placement for a period, such as a homepage takeover.

      Whatever model a campaign is bought on, advertisers compare results by converting everything into effective rates: eCPM (what the spend works out to per thousand impressions), eCPC and eCPA.

      2026: prices are flattening in streaming

      Connected TV once commanded steadily rising CPMs. With Prime Video ads switched on by default in January 2024 and Netflix, Disney+ and others all selling ad tiers, supply is now growing as fast as demand; eMarketer's analysts argue the era of big CTV CPM increases is over.

      Key takeaways

      • Publishers sell access to audiences; advertisers buy it. Ads are one of several ways publishers earn money, and logins have become strategically valuable.
      • Inventory is made of ad slots; creatives must meet each slot's specifications.
      • CPM shifts risk to the advertiser, CPA to the publisher; effective rates let you compare across models.

      A history of advertising technology

      From the first banner in 1994 to agent protocols in 2026: each platform was invented to fix the last one's problem

      Ad tech did not arrive as a designed system. It accumulated, layer by layer, as each new tool solved a problem that the previous tools had created. Understanding that sequence is the fastest way to understand why the ecosystem looks the way it does.

      1994–2000: banners, cookies and the first ad servers

      Two inventions in 1994 set everything in motion. At Netscape, Lou Montulli and John Giannandrea created the HTTP cookie, a small piece of data a website could store in the browser to remember a visitor, originally for shopping carts. That October, AT&T ran what is generally cited as the first banner ad, on HotWired. Advertisers bought space directly from publishers, much as they bought print ads, and publishers placed the ads by hand.

      Hand-placing ads did not scale, so ad servers appeared from 1995: software that stored creatives, decided which ad to show in each slot, and counted impressions and clicks. DoubleClick, founded in 1996, became the leading ad server and also built one of the first ad networks. Search advertising began the same year, and in 2000 Google launched AdWords, moving it to cost-per-click pricing in 2002. In 2003 Google acquired Applied Semantics and launched AdSense, which let any website show contextually matched Google ads.

      2000–2008: networks, waterfalls and exchanges

      Most publishers could not sell all their inventory directly, so they handed leftovers to ad networks, which aggregated inventory from many sites and resold it to advertisers. But each network bought only the parts that matched its campaigns, so publishers worked with several, calling them one after another in a fixed priority order: the waterfall. Advertisers had the mirror-image problem: to reach enough people they bought through several networks, often paying multiple times for the same users with little visibility into where ads ran.

      Two fixes emerged. Network optimisers, the ancestors of today's supply-side platforms (SSPs), managed many networks on a publisher's behalf and picked the best one per impression. Ad exchanges went further, creating marketplaces where individual impressions could be bought and sold. 2007 brought a consolidation wave: Google bought DoubleClick for $3.1 billion, Yahoo bought Right Media, Microsoft bought AdECN. Around 2008–2009 came real-time bidding (RTB): auctioning each impression individually, in real time, as the page loads. Demand-side platforms (DSPs) such as MediaMath appeared to let advertisers bid in those auctions using their own data.

      2009–2021: programmatic at scale

      The next decade built out programmatic advertising and then confronted its side effects. Mobile apps brought their own ad networks and identifiers. Data management platforms let advertisers and publishers build audiences from cookies. Around 2014–2015 publishers, frustrated that the waterfall and Google's own exchange left money on the table, adopted header bidding, letting many exchanges bid at once; the open-source Prebid.js (2015) became its standard. Fraud and opacity prompted transparency standards: ads.txt (2017), then sellers.json and the SupplyChain object (2019). In 2019 Google's exchange moved from second-price to first-price auctions, completing an industry-wide shift.

      Privacy became the dominant theme. Safari's Intelligent Tracking Prevention (2017) began limiting third-party cookies; the EU's General Data Protection Regulation took effect in May 2018; California's CCPA in January 2020. In January 2020 Google announced it would phase out third-party cookies in Chrome. Apple's App Tracking Transparency (April 2021) required apps to ask permission before tracking users, sharply reducing access to the iPhone's advertising identifier.

      2022–2026: courts, concentration, Apple and AI

      The most recent era overturned several assumptions. Ad-supported streaming went mainstream with Netflix and Disney+ ad tiers (late 2022) and Prime Video ads by default (January 2024). The US Department of Justice sued Google over ad tech in January 2023 and won a liability ruling in April 2025. An influential 2023 study by the Association of National Advertisers documented how much programmatic spend was wasted on "made-for-advertising" sites. Chrome abandoned cookie deprecation in July 2024 and retired most of the Privacy Sandbox in October 2025. Microsoft closed the DSP it had bought as Xandr (formerly AppNexus) in February 2026. AI moved into the buying process through agent protocols launched in late 2025, and ChatGPT began showing ads in February 2026. In September 2026, a court ordered behavioural rather than structural remedies against Google's ad tech business, and Apple's Safari 27 began blocking named identity and ad tech domains.

      Key takeaways

      • Ad servers fixed manual placement; networks fixed unsold inventory; SSPs and exchanges fixed the network waterfall; RTB and DSPs enabled impression-level buying.
      • Header bidding and transparency standards were responses to opacity and lost revenue in programmatic.
      • 2022–2026 was shaped less by the end of cookies than by antitrust, streaming, waste, Apple and AI.

      Platforms and intermediaries

      Who sits between the advertiser's budget and the publisher's page, what each does, and how each gets paid

      Between an advertiser and a publisher sits a chain of specialised companies. Some act for the buyer, some for the seller, some supply data or quality checks, and a few giant companies run the whole chain inside their own walls.

      The buy side

      Advertisers and agencies. Advertisers set goals and budgets. Agencies plan and buy on their behalf; the six large holding companies became five in November 2025 when Omnicom completed its acquisition of IPG (chapter 15). An agency trading desk is the programmatic buying unit inside an agency, and many large brands have built their own in-house desks.

      Advertiser ad servers (third-party ad servers) store the advertiser's creatives, serve them into whichever placements were bought, and count impressions and clicks independently of the publisher. Examples include Google's Campaign Manager 360 and Flashtalking, which Mediaocean combined with Innovid after acquiring it in 2025.

      Demand-side platform (DSP)Software that lets a buyer bid on impressions across many exchanges and SSPs from one interface, using targeting rules, data and optimisation algorithms.

      Demand-side platforms are the buyer's engine for programmatic media. A DSP receives billions of bid requests a day, evaluates each one against the buyer's campaigns, decides whether and how much to bid, and reports results. Major DSPs in 2026 include Google's Display & Video 360, The Trade Desk, Amazon DSP and Yahoo DSP. The market changed sharply after 2022:

      • MediaMath, one of the first DSPs, filed for bankruptcy in June 2023; its assets were bought by Infillion.
      • Microsoft stopped supporting buying through its Xandr DSP (Microsoft Invest) on 28 February 2026, saying the current DSP model did not fit the "conversational and agentic" advertising it wanted to build. It kept its sell-side products.
      • Amazon DSP became the most aggressive challenger, combining low fees with supply no one else had: a shared identifier with Roku covering an estimated 80 million US CTV households (June 2025) and direct access to Netflix inventory in 11 markets from late 2025.
      • The Trade Desk, the largest independent DSP, moved its customers to an AI-driven platform called Kokai, launched OpenPath (direct publisher connections) and a TV operating system (Ventura), but reported a year-over-year revenue decline in Q3 2026.

      Ad networks still exist, particularly in mobile apps and for small publishers, but have largely merged into SSPs and exchanges for web inventory.

      The sell side

      Publishers and AdOps. A publisher's advertising operations team sets up campaigns, manages ad tags and auctions, troubleshoots delivery and reconciles reports.

      The publisher ad server (first-party ad server) is the decision engine for every slot on the publisher's properties. It holds directly sold campaigns, runs programmatic competition, and makes the final call on which ad appears. Google Ad Manager, whose ad server is still widely called DFP (DoubleClick for Publishers), dominates; a US court found in April 2025 that Google had illegally monopolised this market (chapter 6).

      Supply-side platform (SSP)Software that lets a publisher offer each impression to many buyers at once, enforce price floors and quality rules, and manage deals.

      SSPs and ad exchanges are the publisher's equivalent of the DSP. An exchange is the marketplace where bid requests meet bids; an SSP is the publisher-facing software around it. Today the terms are used almost interchangeably. Leading names include Google AdX, Magnite, PubMatic, Index Exchange and OpenX, plus Microsoft Monetize.

      The biggest sell-side change since 2022 is curation. Instead of passing raw inventory to DSPs and letting buyers apply their own data, SSPs and data partners now package audiences together with supply, for example "auto intenders on premium news sites in India", and sell the package as a deal ID that any DSP can buy. Supporters see curation as the sell side reclaiming value; The Trade Desk argues it hides fees inside the supply path. Microsoft kept its Curate product even as it shut its DSP.

      Data and quality intermediaries

      • Data management platforms (DMPs) collected cookie-based data and built audience segments. As cross-site data has become harder to collect and use, the category has largely been absorbed into customer data platforms, data clean rooms and identity providers (chapter 11).
      • Data brokers and identity providers such as LiveRamp (which Publicis agreed to buy in May 2026), Experian and Acxiom (now part of Omnicom) assemble and license data and identity graphs.
      • Data clean rooms let two parties combine data for matching and measurement without either seeing the other's raw records: Google Ads Data Hub, Amazon Marketing Cloud, AWS Clean Rooms, Snowflake, LiveRamp, InfoSum (chapter 11 covers DMPs, CDPs and clean rooms in depth).
      • Verification and measurement vendors such as DoubleVerify and Integral Ad Science check that ads were viewable, brand-safe and seen by humans. DoubleVerify added AI bid optimisation by buying Scibids in 2023.
      • Creative tools range from dynamic creative optimisation, which assembles ads from components in real time, to generative AI built into Google, Meta and Amazon's platforms that produces images, video and copy.

      Walled gardens and retail media networks

      A walled garden is a company that owns a large logged-in audience and sells access only through its own tools, keeping user-level data inside: Google (Search, YouTube), Meta (Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp), Amazon, TikTok, and increasingly Netflix and OpenAI. Because they know who their users are, walled gardens have been least affected by browser privacy changes, and they have taken most of the market's growth: WARC calculates that Alphabet, Amazon and Meta take 59.7% of ad spend outside China in 2026, up from 56.1% in 2025.

      Retail media networks are the fastest-growing kind of walled garden: retailers selling ads on their own sites and apps, and increasingly elsewhere using their shopper data (chapter 5).

      How intermediaries make money

      Most ad tech companies earn a percentage of the media that flows through them. DSPs charge buyers a platform fee, typically a percentage of spend, plus fees for data and add-ons; SSPs take a revenue share from the publisher's side of each transaction. Ad networks and some agencies buy media and resell it at a margin (principal-based buying). Data providers charge a CPM or a share of media cost. Verification vendors charge per thousand impressions measured. Because each hop takes a cut, the industry has spent a decade on supply-path optimisation (SPO): buyers cutting the number of intermediaries between their budget and the publisher. Chapter 13 shows how much of a dollar survives the trip.

      Standards bodies

      Interoperability depends on shared standards. The IAB and IAB Tech Lab maintain OpenRTB (the auction protocol), VAST (video ads), ads.txt, sellers.json, the Global Privacy Platform, and since 2026 the Agentic Advertising Management Protocols (AAMP). IAB Europe runs the Transparency & Consent Framework. The MRC accredits measurement. Prebid.org governs the open-source header bidding code that a US court has now made central to Google's remedies, and also operates the UID2 identifier. A new body, AgenticAdvertising.Org, governs the Advertising Context Protocol (AdCP).

      Key takeaways

      • DSPs act for buyers, SSPs for sellers; the publisher ad server makes the final decision.
      • Since 2022, Xandr's DSP and MediaMath are gone, Amazon DSP has risen, and curation has blurred the line between SSP and data provider.
      • Walled gardens hold close to 60% of spend outside China because they own logged-in audiences.
      • Every hop takes a fee, which is why buyers optimise their supply paths.

      Ad formats and channels

      What ads look like, where they run, and where the money is going in 2026

      An ad is defined by two things: its medium (text, image, video, audio, interactive) and the channel it runs in (web, app, social, search, TV, outdoor, retail, AI chat). Each combination has its own technology, standards and economics.

      Formats

      Text and image ads are the oldest formats: text ads in search results and contextual networks; image banners in standard IAB sizes, now usually HTML5 so they can animate and resize responsively.

      Native ads match the look of the surrounding content: sponsored posts in a social feed, "recommended" article links, promoted listings in a marketplace or retailer search. Native is delivered as assets (headline, image, description, brand name, call to action) that the publisher renders in its own style, following the OpenRTB Native specification.

      Video ads run in-stream (before, during or after video content) or out-stream (in a video player inserted into an article). Video ad delivery relies on a family of IAB standards:

      • VAST (Video Ad Serving Template) is an XML document telling the video player which media file to play and which tracking URLs to call at events such as start, quartiles and completion. VAST 4.x is current.
      • VMAP (Video Multiple Ad Playlist) describes where ad breaks fall in a piece of content.
      • VPAID, which let ads run code inside the player for interactivity and measurement, has been phased out because it slowed players and was a security risk. Its jobs are now split between SIMID (interactive overlays) and the Open Measurement SDK (verification).

      In streaming TV, ads are increasingly stitched into the stream on the server (server-side ad insertion, SSAI), so they play as smoothly as the show and are harder to block.

      Rich media covers ads that expand, play video, include games or collect information in-unit. Audio ads run in music streaming, podcasts and digital radio, sold both by direct deals and programmatically, and measured by downloads or listens.

      Channels

      Web. Display and video on websites, bought directly or programmatically. The open web is now a minority of digital spend and faces two pressures: browser restrictions on identity (chapter 10) and AI answer engines that reduce clicks to content sites (chapter 16).

      Mobile apps. In-app ads are served through SDKs (software kits built into apps) and mediation platforms that run auctions among ad networks. Formats include banners, interstitials, native and rewarded video. Identity relies on device advertising IDs, constrained on iPhone by Apple's App Tracking Transparency. AppLovin became the dominant in-app ad platform, with 2025 revenue of $5.48 billion, up 70%.

      Social. Meta, TikTok, Snap, Pinterest, LinkedIn and X sell ads in feeds, stories and short video through self-serve platforms with their own targeting and measurement. TikTok's US operations moved to a joint venture in January 2026 under a 2024 law forcing a sale; ByteDance keeps 19.9%, and questions remain in Congress about how complete the separation is. pending

      Search. Ads matched to the words people search for, sold by auction on a cost-per-click basis, still the largest single format: WPP Media forecasts search (including generative search) at 21.8% of global ad revenue in 2026. Search is changing as Google's AI Overviews and AI Mode answer questions directly; both now carry ads.

      Connected TV and streaming (CTV/OTT). CTV means ads on internet-connected TV sets; OTT means video delivered over the internet rather than cable or satellite, on any screen. Streaming services, smart TV makers (Samsung, LG, Roku, Vizio) and free ad-supported streaming channels (FAST) all sell ads.

      Digital out-of-home (DOOH). Digital screens in public places: billboards, transit, malls, airports, elevators. DOOH is increasingly bought programmatically, with audiences estimated from mobility data rather than counted per person. It has recovered strongly from the pandemic collapse described in early-2020s sources.

      Retail media. Ads sold by retailers on their own sites, apps and in-store screens, and increasingly "off-site" on other channels using their purchase data. Because the retailer sees both the ad and the sale, it can show advertisers a closed loop from impression to purchase.

      AI assistants. OpenAI began showing ads to logged-in adult users of ChatGPT's Free and Go tiers in the US on 9 February 2026, opened a self-serve Ads Manager with CPC bidding in May, added display ads beside image generation in October, and reached 89 countries, including India, by 21 October. Ad measurement partners include AppsFlyer, Adjust, Branch, Singular and Kochava; DoubleVerify and IAS run brand-suitability pilots.

      Where the money is going

      Forecasters measure the market differently (some count media owners' revenue, some advertisers' spend; some include China or US political ads), so their numbers should not be mixed. Each figure below names its source.

      The streaming market reorganised quickly. Netflix launched its ad tier in November 2022 with Microsoft as exclusive technology and sales partner, then replaced Microsoft with its own Netflix Ads Suite during 2025 and opened access to The Trade Desk, Google DV360, Yahoo DSP and Amazon DSP. Disney+ launched ads in December 2022. Walmart bought TV maker Vizio in December 2024, giving its retail media business a TV operating system; its global ad business reached nearly $6.4 billion in fiscal 2026.

      Key takeaways

      • Video runs on VAST; VPAID has been replaced by SIMID and Open Measurement.
      • CTV, retail media and AI assistants became first-class channels between 2022 and 2026.
      • Amazon dominates retail media; Netflix, Prime Video and Disney+ made premium streaming programmatic.
      • Never mix figures from different forecasters in one comparison.

      Ad serving

      How an ad server decides, delivers and counts, the tags that connect it to pages, and the court ruling that now governs the biggest one

      An ad server is the system that answers the question "which ad goes here, right now?" and then makes sure the answer is delivered and counted. Every other platform in the chain ultimately feeds a decision made by an ad server.

      From insertion orders to automated decisions

      In the earliest workflow, an advertiser and publisher signed an insertion order (IO) specifying placements, dates, impressions and price. The advertiser sent creatives, often as a spreadsheet of tags; the publisher's AdOps team keyed the campaign into its ad server; and reports went back and forth by email. That workflow survives for direct-sold sponsorships, but most decisions are now automated: the ad server holds many campaigns and evaluates each request against them in milliseconds.

      Publisher and advertiser ad servers

      The publisher ad server manages the publisher's inventory. For each request it considers directly sold campaigns (line items with targeting, priority, pacing and frequency caps), programmatic deals, and open-auction demand, and picks the winner. It also enforces rules: competitive exclusions, brand blocks, floor prices.

      The advertiser ad server sits on the other side. When the publisher's server chooses a campaign bought from an advertiser, it often returns a tag that calls the advertiser's server, which then picks the specific creative (for example, rotating variants or choosing by location) and counts the impression in the advertiser's own records. This double counting is deliberate: it lets both parties verify delivery, and it is the source of the reporting discrepancies covered in chapter 8.

      Ad tags

      An ad tag is the snippet of code placed on a page or in an app that requests an ad. There are several kinds, each a trade-off between flexibility and safety.

      JavaScript tags run code directly on the page. They can read the page context, detect screen size, run auctions and render rich formats, which is why most modern setups use them (Google Publisher Tag and Prebid.js are both JavaScript). The downside is that third-party code runs with access to the page: it can slow it down, break layouts, or, if compromised, deliver malware.

      <script async src="https://securepubads.g.doubleclick.net/tag/js/gpt.js"></script> <div id="slot-mpu"></div> <script> googletag.cmd.push(function () { googletag.defineSlot('/1234/news/markets', [300, 250], 'slot-mpu') .addService(googletag.pubads()); googletag.enableServices(); googletag.display('slot-mpu'); }); </script>

      Iframe tags load the ad inside an inline frame, a separate document isolated from the page. That protects the page but blinds the ad: it cannot see where it sits or whether it is visible, and it cannot expand. SafeFrame, an IAB standard, is an iframe plus a controlled API through which the ad can ask the host page for its position, viewability and permission to expand. Ad servers now render most third-party creatives in SafeFrames by default.

      VAST tags are URLs that return VAST XML to a video player (chapter 5). Image (IMG) tags are the simplest: an image link that serves a static banner and counts the request as an impression. They work almost anywhere, including email, but cannot run auctions, rotate creatives intelligently, or measure viewability.

      Whatever the tag type, the returned ad markup does two jobs: load the creative into the slot, and fire tracking for the impression, verification and viewability vendors.

      Google's ad server on trial

      Google Ad Manager combines the market's dominant publisher ad server (DFP) with its own exchange (AdX). For years publishers complained that the integration gave AdX advantages no rival exchange could match. Three auction features were at the heart of the complaints: First Look (AdX could see and take impressions before other demand), Last Look (AdX could see rivals' header bids and then beat them), and Unified Pricing Rules (which stopped publishers setting different floors for different exchanges).

      2023–2026: US v. Google (ad tech)

      The DOJ and several states sued on 24 January 2023. On 17 April 2025, Judge Leonie Brinkema found Google liable for monopolising publisher ad servers and ad exchanges for open-web display, and for unlawfully tying DFP to AdX. On 2 September 2026 she rejected the DOJ's request to divest AdX or DFP and instead ordered six years of behavioural remedies under a monitor: DFP and AdX must interoperate with Prebid; publishers can export their data; Google's buying tools may not favour its own ad tech; First Look, Last Look and Unified Pricing Rules may not return; tying is banned. Most measures take effect within about 15 months. final judgment, appeal

      Private claims follow: on 30 September 2026 a New York court allowed a class of about 5,000 publishers to pursue roughly $1.7 billion, alongside separate suits by USA Today and the Daily Mail. In Europe, the Commission fined Google €2.95 billion on 5 September 2025 and has said a structural remedy may still be needed.

      Key takeaways

      • Publisher ad servers make the final decision; advertiser ad servers pick creatives and count independently.
      • JavaScript tags are flexible but risky; iframes and SafeFrame isolate ads; VAST serves video.
      • Google's integrated ad server and exchange were found unlawful; the remedy opens them to Prebid and removes their auction privileges rather than splitting them up.

      Targeting and budget control

      Choosing who sees an ad, where and when, and controlling how fast and how often money is spent

      Targeting decides which impressions a campaign should buy. Budget control decides how much to spend on them, how fast, and how many times the same person should see the ad.

      Contextual targeting

      Contextual targeting matches ads to the content around them: a running-shoe ad on a marathon training article. It needs no information about the person, which is why it has returned to favour as cross-site identity has weakened.

      The process is straightforward. A crawler scans a publisher's pages and classifies them, traditionally by keywords and the IAB Content Taxonomy, and increasingly with language and vision models that understand meaning, sentiment and suitability rather than just words. When a page loads, its URL and categories travel with the ad request through the SSP to DSPs, which bid on pages whose context matches their campaigns. Signals include:

      • Keywords and topics extracted from the page.
      • Ad slot and position: above or below the fold, size, in-article or sidebar.
      • The URL and domain, used for allow-lists and block-lists.

      Environmental targeting

      Other signals describe the situation rather than the person: location, derived from the IP address (country, region, city) or, in apps with permission, GPS; device, browser and operating system, parsed from the user agent and client hints; connection type and carrier; and time of day and day of week (day-parting), useful for restaurants at lunchtime or B2B ads during working hours. IP-based location is becoming less precise as Apple's iCloud Private Relay and similar services mask addresses.

      Audience targeting

      Behavioural targeting uses a person's past actions: pages visited, searches, purchases, app usage. It works in three steps: collect events tied to an identifier, group identifiers into segments ("sports fans", "recent car-insurance browsers"), and activate those segments in buying platforms. It depends on recognising the same person across sites, so it is the method most affected by cookie restrictions, ATT and privacy law; GDPR generally requires consent for it.

      Retargeting shows ads to people who have already interacted with the advertiser, such as cart abandoners. A tag on the advertiser's site records the visit against an ID; when that ID later appears in a bid request elsewhere, the DSP bids. Retargeting through third-party cookies now works only in Chrome; elsewhere it relies on logged-in walled gardens, server-side data and first-party IDs.

      Demographic targeting uses age, gender, income, household composition and similar attributes, either declared by users in logged-in platforms or inferred. Sensitive categories are increasingly off-limits: the EU's Digital Services Act has, since February 2024, banned online platforms from targeting ads using sensitive data such as health or religion, or at minors.

      2026: targeting handed to the machine

      The biggest change is who does the targeting. Google's Performance Max (launched 2021) and AI Max for Search (2025), and Meta's Advantage+ campaigns, ask advertisers for goals, budgets and creative assets, then choose audiences and placements algorithmically, with limited visibility into how. Meta has said it wants advertisers to be able to fully automate creative, targeting and budgeting by the end of 2026. In October 2026 Google updated its privacy policy so that inferences from connected Gmail, Drive and Photos data can shape Search ads. EU response

      Controlling the budget: pacing

      A campaign's budget is set per day or per flight. Pacing controls how fast it is spent. ASAP (accelerated) pacing bids on every eligible impression until the money runs out, which is useful for a flash sale but often exhausts the budget early in the day and misses later audiences. Uniform (even) pacing spreads spend across the period, often weighted by expected traffic. DSPs adjust bids continuously to stay on their pacing curve.

      Controlling exposure: frequency capping

      Frequency capping limits how many times one person sees an ad in a period, for example three impressions per 24 hours. It prevents annoyance, stops budget being wasted on the same few heavy users, and spreads reach across more people. It requires recognising the person across impressions, so caps are reliable inside walled gardens and logged-in streaming services but leaky on the open web, especially in Safari, where a user without a stable ID looks like a new person every time.

      Key takeaways

      • Contextual and environmental targeting need no personal data and have revived; behavioural targeting and retargeting depend on identity and consent.
      • AI campaign types now make most targeting decisions inside the walled gardens.
      • Pacing controls speed of spend; frequency capping controls repetition, and only works as well as identity does.

      Tracking and reporting

      How impressions, clicks and conversions are counted, why platforms disagree, and why measurement moved to servers

      Advertising runs on counts. Publishers are paid on impressions, performance buyers on clicks and conversions, and every count is produced by a different piece of code that can fail or be blocked.

      Impression tracking

      An impression can be counted in two ways. In the first, the ad server logs that it served the ad when it returned the markup. In the second, the creative or page fires a tracking pixel (a request for a tiny invisible image, or a JavaScript beacon) when the ad actually renders. The IAB guidelines favour counting at render ("begin to render"), as close to the user as possible, because served ads can fail to load. Verification vendors then add separate signals for viewability and invalid traffic.

      Click tracking

      When a user clicks an ad, the click usually passes through one or more click trackers: redirect URLs that log the click and forward the browser to the next hop and finally to the landing page. A typical chain goes through the publisher's ad server, the advertiser's ad server, and sometimes an attribution vendor.

      Ad servers fill in macros, placeholders in the URL replaced at serve time, so each hop knows which campaign, creative, site and click it is handling:

      https://adserver.example/click?cid=${CAMPAIGN_ID}&crid=${CREATIVE_ID}&site=${DOMAIN} &redirect=https%3A%2F%2Fshop.example%2Fsneakers%3Futm_source%3Ddisplay

      Landing-page URLs commonly carry UTM parameters (campaign labels for analytics tools) and platform click IDs such as Google's gclid or Meta's fbclid, which tie a visit to a specific ad click.

      Conversion tracking

      A conversion is credited to an ad either as a click-through conversion (the user clicked, then converted within a window such as 30 days) or a view-through conversion (the user saw but did not click the ad, then converted within a shorter window such as one day). Click-through and view-through conversion rates are conversions divided by clicks and by impressions respectively.

      There are two ways to record conversions:

      • Client-side pixels. A tag on the confirmation page fires to each ad platform, which matches it to the click or impression using a cookie or click ID. Easy to deploy, but blocked by ad blockers and browser privacy features, and dependent on identifiers that are disappearing.
      • Server-side (server-to-server) tracking. The advertiser's server sends conversion events directly to the platform's API, with identifiers such as a click ID or a hashed email. Affiliate networks and mobile app attribution always worked this way through postbacks. Since 2022 it has become the standard everywhere: Meta's Conversions API, Google's Enhanced Conversions, TikTok's Events API, and in 2026 an OpenAI Conversions API for ChatGPT ads.
      2023–2025: browsers close the gaps

      Apple's Link Tracking Protection (2023) strips click IDs in private contexts; Safari 26 (2025) applies Advanced Fingerprinting Protection to all browsing by default. Google's Attribution Reporting API, intended as a privacy-preserving browser replacement for conversion tracking, was retired in October 2025. Server-side events with first-party identifiers, plus statistical modelling, are now the main way conversions are measured.

      Reporting

      Reports are built from metrics (impressions, viewable impressions, clicks, conversions, spend, revenue) and dimensions that slice them (date, campaign, creative, site, device, country). Derived metrics compare performance:

      • eCPM = spend ÷ impressions × 1,000; eCPC = spend ÷ clicks; eCPA = spend ÷ conversions.
      • ROI = (conversion value − spend) ÷ spend × 100%; ROAS = conversion value ÷ spend.

      Technical details matter: data arrives with delays (conversions can trickle in for weeks), platforms report in different time zones, and raw logs are kept only for a retention period, after which only aggregates remain.

      Discrepancies

      Two systems counting the same campaign rarely agree. Common causes include implementation errors (a tag placed twice, a missing macro), configuration differences (time zones, counting served versus rendered, invalid-traffic filtering), and client-side losses (users leaving before a tracker fires, ad blockers, network failures, redirects stripped by browsers). Buyers and sellers agree in advance which system is the "source of truth" for billing and what discrepancy tolerance is acceptable, commonly around 10%, and reconcile beyond that.

      Key takeaways

      • Impressions are best counted at render; clicks pass through redirect trackers with macros; conversions are click-through or view-through.
      • Server-side conversion APIs replaced browser pixels as the reliable measurement path.
      • Discrepancies are normal; agree the source of truth and a tolerance up front.

      Media buying: programmatic, RTB, header bidding and deals

      The ways media is bought and sold, how a real-time auction works, and the agent protocols layering on top

      "Programmatic" means using software, rather than people exchanging emails and spreadsheets, to execute media transactions. It does not necessarily mean auctions, and it does not necessarily mean the open market.

      Manual versus programmatic

      In manual buying, a buyer researches publishers, requests proposals, negotiates prices, signs an insertion order, sends creatives, and waits for the publisher to traffic the campaign and send reports. Optimisation means calling the publisher to move budget. It is slow and labour-intensive, but suits large sponsorships and custom content.

      In programmatic buying, the buyer's platform and the seller's platform exchange machine-readable offers and creatives, so campaigns launch and adjust without manual trafficking. Optimisation becomes algorithmic: the DSP raises bids where results are good and withdraws where they are poor, impression by impression.

      The four transaction types

      TypeInventoryPriceWho can buy
      Programmatic guaranteed (PG)Reserved, guaranteed volumeFixed, negotiatedOne buyer
      Preferred dealNot guaranteed; first refusalFixed, negotiatedOne buyer
      Private marketplace (PMP), including curated dealsNot guaranteedAuction, with a floorInvited buyers
      Open auction (open RTB)Not guaranteedAuctionAnyone connected

      The first two are often grouped as programmatic direct: a negotiated deal executed through programmatic pipes, identified by a deal ID that the SSP attaches to bid requests so the right buyer recognises and bids on them. Private marketplaces give publishers a way to sell premium inventory or audiences to selected buyers at higher prices. Since around 2024, curated deals have become a major PMP variant: an SSP or data company bundles inventory from many publishers with audience data and sells it as a single deal ID to any DSP.

      How real-time bidding works

      OpenRTBThe IAB Tech Lab protocol that defines bid requests and responses. Version 2.6 added fields such as the SupplyChain object at source.schain and privacy signals at regs.gpp.

      When a page or app with programmatic inventory loads, the SSP builds a bid request describing the opportunity and sends it to many DSPs at once. The request includes:

      • The impression: slot size or video parameters, position, floor price, any deal IDs.
      • The context: site or app, page URL, content categories.
      • The device: type, OS, browser, approximate location, connection.
      • The user: any IDs the publisher can share (a buyer-synced cookie ID, a first-party or universal ID), subject to consent.
      • Regulations and supply chain: privacy and consent signals, and the list of every company that handled the request.
      • tmax: the deadline, commonly 100–300 ms.

      Each DSP evaluates the request against its campaigns, looks up any data it holds on the user, predicts the value of the impression (for example the probability of a click or conversion), and returns a bid response with a price, the creative markup or a URL to fetch it, and the advertiser's domain. The SSP discards late or invalid bids, runs the auction and passes the winner to the publisher's ad server, which compares it with direct campaigns and other demand. The whole process usually takes well under a second.

      Auction rules: second price, first price, shading and floors

      Programmatic auctions originally used the second-price rule: the highest bidder wins but pays one cent more than the second-highest bid. In theory this encourages bidders to bid their true value. In practice, as header bidding layered several auctions on top of one another, the rule broke down: a "second price" in one auction could be compared with first prices elsewhere, and opaque floors let sellers manipulate the clearing price. By 2019, when Google's exchange switched, the industry had moved to first-price auctions: the winner pays exactly what it bid.

      First-price auctions push the burden of not overpaying onto buyers. DSPs responded with bid shading: algorithms that estimate the lowest price likely to still win, based on historical clearing prices, and bid that instead of the full value.

      Sellers protect their prices with floors. A hard floor is a minimum below which no bid is accepted. A soft floor was a second-price-era mechanism in which bids below it could still win but paid their own bid. Floors can vary by buyer, deal, geography or ad size; Google's Unified Pricing Rules, which prevented publishers from setting different floors for AdX and rival exchanges, were barred by the 2026 court remedies.

      The waterfall

      Before header bidding, publishers offered unsold impressions to demand sources one at a time in a fixed order, the waterfall (or daisy chain). The ad server first tried direct campaigns, then passed the impression to the network ranked highest on historical average CPM, with a floor; if that network did not fill, it moved down to the next, and so on, ending with remnant networks or house ads.

      The waterfall forces a dilemma between price and fill. High floors at the top tiers earn more per impression but cause more passbacks, latency and unsold inventory; low floors fill more but leave money on the table. Worse, ranking by historical averages means a buyer lower in the chain who would pay more for this particular impression never gets asked.

      Header bidding

      Header bidding fixes that by asking many demand partners for bids simultaneously before the ad server makes its decision. A wrapper script, almost always the open-source Prebid.js on the web (Prebid Mobile in apps), calls each SSP's adapter in parallel, collects bids within a timeout, and passes the best ones into the ad server as key-values, where they compete against direct campaigns and the ad server's own exchange.

      Header bidding can run in two places:

      • Client-side: the user's browser calls each bidder. It gives each bidder direct access to its own cookies, so match rates and bids are higher, but every extra bidder adds network requests, latency and page weight.
      • Server-side: the browser makes one call to a server (Prebid Server, Amazon's Transparent Ad Marketplace, Google's Open Bidding), which fans out to bidders. It is faster and scales to more partners, and is the norm in apps and CTV, but bidders lose direct cookie access, so match rates are lower unless IDs are passed.

      Header bidding raised publisher revenue and broke Google's exclusive first look at inventory, at the cost of complexity and latency. Prebid.js 10 shipped in 2025.

      2026: Prebid becomes court-mandated infrastructure

      The remedies in US v. Google (ad tech) require Google's ad server and exchange to interoperate with Prebid for six years. Open-source header bidding, once a workaround, is now the neutral auction layer the competition remedy is built on.

      Agentic buying

      The newest buying layer uses AI agents. Instead of a person configuring line items in a DSP, a buyer agent given a brief and budget can discover inventory, request proposals from seller agents, negotiate deal terms, launch and adjust campaigns, and report back, all through machine-readable protocols. Two families of standards compete:

      • Advertising Context Protocol (AdCP): launched 15 October 2025 as the Ad Context Protocol by a coalition including Yahoo, PubMatic, Optable, Scope3, Swivel and Triton Digital, built on Anthropic's Model Context Protocol (MCP) with Google's Agent2Agent (A2A) as a second transport, and governed by AgenticAdvertising.Org. Version 3.0 (release candidate March 2026) adopted the new name.
      • IAB Tech Lab's AAMP (Agentic Advertising Management Protocols): the Tech Lab released its Agentic RTB Framework for comment in November 2025, aiming to cut bidding latency by about 80% by running bidder components in containers inside exchanges; it published an agentic roadmap extending OpenRTB, AdCOM, VAST and OpenDirect in January 2026, named the umbrella AAMP in February, and released AAMP 2.0 with buyer and seller agent SDKs in June 2026. LiveRamp donated its User Context Protocol to the effort.

      By August 2026 the Tech Lab counted 13 functions the two specifications share. Whether they converge or fight a standards war is unresolved. pending Buyers' adoption in 2026 has been exploratory; the impression itself still clears through OpenRTB.

      Key takeaways

      • Programmatic covers guaranteed deals, preferred deals, private marketplaces (including curated deals) and the open auction.
      • RTB auctions are first-price; buyers shade bids, sellers set floors.
      • Header bidding replaced the waterfall by asking all bidders at once; Prebid is now required interoperability for Google.
      • Agent protocols (AdCP, AAMP) add a negotiation layer above OpenRTB; the standards race is still open.

      User identification

      Cookies, syncing, fingerprints, device IDs and universal IDs, and why identity split into two worlds

      Identity is what lets ad tech recognise that the person on this page is the same person who saw an ad yesterday, abandoned a cart last week, or bought something today. Targeting, retargeting, frequency capping, attribution and fraud detection all depend on it.

      Cookies

      A cookie is a small text file a website asks the browser to store and send back on later requests to the same domain. Cookies hold session state, logins, preferences and, for advertising, identifiers.

      What matters is whose domain sets the cookie. A first-party cookie belongs to the site in the address bar: news.example setting a cookie for news.example. A third-party cookie belongs to another domain whose content is loaded on the page, such as an ad server's pixel at adtech.example. Because the same ad tech domain is loaded on thousands of sites, its third-party cookie lets it recognise a browser everywhere, which is what made cross-site tracking possible.

      Cookies also differ by lifetime (session cookies vanish when the browser closes; persistent cookies have an expiry date) and by attributes. Chrome's 2020 SameSite change made cookies first-party only by default unless explicitly marked SameSite=None; Secure.

      Cookie syncing

      Every ad tech company has its own cookie ID for the same browser, and none can read another's cookies. To trade data or bid on a user, they must map their IDs to each other. Cookie syncing (ID matching) does this with a chain of redirects: one platform's pixel calls the partner's endpoint with its own ID in the URL; the partner reads its own cookie and stores the pair in a match table.

      Syncing has costs. Each sync is extra network traffic on the user's device; match rates are never complete; every sync shares an identifier with another company, which privacy law treats as personal data processing. And it depends entirely on third-party cookies, so it fails in Safari and Firefox.

      Other ways to recognise a browser

      • Local storage (HTML5 localStorage and sessionStorage) can hold IDs; browsers now partition it by site, like cookies.
      • Cookie respawning uses copies of an ID stored elsewhere (local storage, cache, ETags, the older Flash cookies) to recreate a deleted cookie. Evercookies took this to the extreme, hiding an ID in a dozen places. Both are widely treated as abusive and are blocked by modern browsers.
      • Device fingerprinting combines many attributes (IP address, user agent, screen size, fonts, time zone, hardware and graphics characteristics) into a probabilistic identifier that survives cookie deletion. Users cannot see or clear it, which is why browsers fight it: Safari 26 applies Advanced Fingerprinting Protection to all browsing, and Firefox and Brave randomise or limit signals.
      February 2025: Google reverses on fingerprinting

      From 16 February 2025, Google's ad platform policies allow advertisers using its products to use fingerprinting-type techniques such as IP addresses and device signals, which Google had previously called incompatible with user choice. The UK Information Commissioner's Office called the change irresponsible. The browser does not block these signals; the policy now permits their use.

      Mobile identification

      Mobile web browsers behave like desktop browsers, but in-app advertising uses advertising IDs supplied by the operating system: Apple's IDFA and Google's Android Advertising ID (GAID/AAID). These resettable IDs are passed in bid requests and attribution calls, giving apps a cross-app identifier without cookies.

      Apple's App Tracking Transparency (iOS 14.5, April 2021) requires apps to get permission through a system prompt before accessing the IDFA or tracking users across other companies' apps and sites. Most users decline, so on iPhone the IDFA is unavailable for the majority of traffic. Google had planned similar restrictions through Privacy Sandbox on Android, but retired that program in October 2025, so the Android advertising ID continues.

      Matching profiles: deterministic and probabilistic

      Deterministic matching links devices and browsers using an exact shared identifier, usually a login: an email address or phone number, normalised and hashed. It is accurate but limited to authenticated users. Probabilistic matching infers that devices belong to the same person or household from signals such as shared IP addresses, locations and usage patterns. It covers more people but is less accurate and harder to justify under privacy law. Identity graphs combine both to link cookies, mobile IDs, emails, CTV devices and offline records into one profile per person or household.

      Universal and alternative IDs

      Universal IDs aim to give the whole ecosystem one shared identifier per user, reducing the need for syncing. The main ones in 2026:

      • Unified ID 2.0 (UID2): created by The Trade Desk from a hashed and encrypted email or phone number, with a consent and opt-out layer; now operated by Prebid.org. EUID is its European variant.
      • RampID (LiveRamp), ID5, Yahoo ConnectID, and publisher-provided IDs.

      These are passed in the bid request's user.eids field. Seller-defined audiences, an IAB Tech Lab idea from 2022 to let publishers describe audiences with taxonomy labels instead of IDs, never became a mass standard.

      The Chrome cookie saga

      For four years the industry planned for Chrome to remove third-party cookies, as Safari and Firefox already had. Google's Privacy Sandbox was meant to replace their advertising uses with browser APIs: Topics (interest categories computed in the browser), Protected Audience (formerly FLEDGE; on-device remarketing auctions) and Attribution Reporting (aggregated conversion measurement). It did not happen.

      Two browser worlds

      The result is a split. In Chrome, which has most of the global browser market, third-party cookies and syncing still work by default. In Safari, Apple's restrictions keep tightening. With iOS 27 and Safari 27 (released 14 September 2026), Apple began blocking network access to a list of named identity-resolution and ad tech domains. A WebKit bug filed by The Trade Desk on 21 September showed the list included its ad-serving domain and the UID2, LiveRamp, ID5, Permutive and Audigent domains; Google and Meta domains were not on it. Apple removed The Trade Desk's ad domain in the iOS 27.2 beta on 5 October and confirmed the policy in a WebKit post on 19 October, without naming companies. full scope of list

      Key takeaways

      • Third-party cookies and cookie syncing enabled cross-site identity; they still work in Chrome but not in Safari or Firefox.
      • Mobile apps use IDFA and GAID; ATT limits IDFA, while GAID survives.
      • The Privacy Sandbox ad APIs were retired; Apple is now the main identity constraint, blocking named identity vendors in Safari 27.
      • Authenticated first-party data is the only identity approach that works in both worlds.

      Data platforms: DMPs, CDPs and data clean rooms

      How advertising data is collected, unified, shared and activated, why the DMP faded, what a CDP really does, and how clean rooms let companies collaborate without handing over their customers

      Advertising runs on data about people: who they are, what they did, what they bought, which ads they saw. Three generations of platform have been built to manage that data. The data management platform (DMP) organised anonymous cookie data for ad targeting. The customer data platform (CDP) unifies a company's own known customers across every channel. The data clean room lets two or more companies combine what they know without exposing it to each other. Understanding all three, and how they fit together in 2026, is essential to understanding modern advertising.

      11.1 Advertising data11.2 The DMP11.3 The CDP11.4 Data clean rooms11.5 How they fit together

      11.1 Advertising data: kinds, sources and permissions

      Data is used throughout the advertising process: to decide whom to reach (targeting and suppression), what to show them (personalisation), how much to pay (bid valuation), whether it worked (measurement and attribution), and what an audience is worth (publisher pricing). Who collected the data, and with what permission, determines what can legally and practically be done with it.

      By relationship

      • Zero-party data is what people deliberately tell a company: preferences in a quiz, communication choices, stated interests. It is accurate but limited in volume.
      • First-party data is collected directly by a company from its own customers and visitors: website and app behaviour, purchases, subscriptions, loyalty-programme activity, customer service records, CRM data. It is the most accurate and the easiest to justify legally, and since 2022 it has become the foundation of almost every data strategy.
      • Second-party data is another company's first-party data, used through a direct partnership: an airline and a hotel group, a brand and a retailer, an advertiser and a streaming service. Historically shared by exchanging files; today usually combined inside a clean room.
      • Third-party data is collected by companies with no direct relationship to the people concerned, aggregated from many sources (cookie tracking, app SDKs, public records, purchase panels, surveys) and sold as segments or attributes. It offers scale but is often inaccurate, stale and of uncertain consent, and its supply has shrunk sharply under browser restrictions and privacy law.

      By source

      Online sources include web and app analytics, ad impressions and clicks, search queries, forms, email engagement and logged-in activity. Offline sources include point-of-sale systems, call centres, loyalty cards, warranty registrations, direct mail responses and public records. Combining the two gives a fuller picture: the person who saw a TV ad, browsed on a phone, and bought in a store. But it is hard, because the same person exists as a cookie, a mobile advertising ID, an email address, a phone number and a loyalty card number in separate systems that were never designed to talk to each other. This is the data fragmentation problem, and each of the three platforms in this chapter is a different answer to it.

      Permission travels with the data

      Purpose limitationA principle in GDPR, India's DPDP Act and most modern privacy laws: data collected for one purpose cannot simply be reused for another. Consent to receive order updates is not consent to be targeted with ads.

      Under modern privacy law, a record is not just a set of fields; it carries the conditions under which it was collected. A customer who consented to marketing emails may not have consented to their email address being matched with a publisher for ad targeting. A visitor in the EU who rejected advertising cookies cannot be added to a retargeting segment. India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act requires consent that is specific to a stated purpose, and its children's data rules restrict tracking and targeted advertising aimed at under-18s. Every platform in this chapter must therefore store and respect consent and purpose alongside the data itself. A clean room, in particular, does not create permission that was not there when the data was collected.

      11.2 The data management platform

      A DMP collects, organises and activates audience data for advertising, primarily anonymous data keyed to cookie IDs and mobile advertising IDs. DMPs flourished from roughly 2010 to 2020, when third-party cookies made it possible to observe users across many sites and send segments to any DSP. Understanding how they worked explains both their power and their decline.

      Architecture

      A DMP has six layers:

      1. Collection. JavaScript tags on the client's websites, pixels in ads and emails, SDKs in apps, and server-to-server APIs that import data from other systems. Each event (page view, product view, ad impression, click) is recorded against a cookie or device ID.
      2. Identity. A store of the DMP's own cookie IDs, plus match tables linking them to partners' IDs through cookie syncing (chapter 10) and to offline identifiers through onboarding.
      3. Event and profile store. Events are aggregated into profiles per ID: interests, recency and frequency of behaviours, inferred attributes.
      4. Taxonomy. A hierarchy of categories that gives the data a common vocabulary.
      5. Segmentation. A rules engine (and later machine learning) that builds audiences from profile attributes.
      6. Activation and analytics. Connectors that push segments to DSPs, ad servers, SSPs and personalisation tools, plus reports on audience composition and overlap.

      Collection: tags, piggybacking and APIs

      The DMP's container tag was usually placed on every page of a client's site. Besides recording the visit, it could piggyback other tags: load partner pixels that synced IDs with DSPs and data providers, or passed data to them. Piggybacking was efficient (one tag on the page, many partners behind it) but opaque, because the site owner often could not see which companies its visitors' browsers were talking to. That opacity is one reason regulators came to regard ad tech data flows as uncontrolled sharing of personal data. Tags were complemented by APIs: CRM systems, e-commerce platforms and analytics tools sent data to the DMP server-to-server.

      Onboarding offline data

      Data onboarding brings offline records, such as a CRM file of customers, into the online advertising world. The process:

      1. The company uploads a file containing customer identifiers (names, postal addresses, emails, phone numbers) and attributes (loyalty tier, lifetime value).
      2. The onboarder normalises identifiers and replaces them with hashed or tokenised versions, separating direct identifiers from attributes.
      3. It matches the records against its identity graph, which links offline identifiers to cookies and device IDs it has observed (for example, when someone logged into a partner site with the same email).
      4. Matched cookie and device IDs, carrying the CRM attributes, are delivered to the DMP or directly to DSPs and platforms.

      Normalisation, enrichment and profiles

      Data from different sources arrives in different shapes: one system records "Bengaluru", another "Bangalore"; one sends prices in rupees, another in dollars; one calls an event add_to_cart, another basket_add. Normalisation maps everything into a common schema. Enrichment adds derived attributes: inferred gender or age band from browsing, income band from postal code, in-market status from recent behaviour, device and location from the user agent and IP address.

      Profile building groups events by ID into a profile. Profile merging combines profiles believed to belong to the same person, for example a desktop cookie and a mobile cookie that both logged in with the same hashed email, or that a probabilistic graph links. Merging increases reach and frequency control but risks joining people who share a device or network.

      Taxonomies and segments

      A taxonomy organises attributes into a hierarchy, such as Interests › Travel › Beach holidays or In-market › Autos › SUVs. A company's own taxonomy reflects its business; data marketplaces use their own; and the IAB Tech Lab's Audience Taxonomy offers a shared vocabulary so segments from different providers can be compared.

      A segment is a rule over profile attributes and behaviours, usually with time conditions: "visited the SUV pages at least twice in the last 14 days, did not book a test drive, located in Mumbai or Pune". Segments have a membership lifetime (an ID drops out after, say, 30 days without qualifying behaviour) because interest fades and because cookies themselves expire or are deleted.

      Look-alike modelling

      A look-alike (or "similar audience") model expands a small, high-value seed audience, such as recent purchasers, into a larger group of people who resemble it statistically. The model learns which features distinguish the seed from the general population and scores everyone else. The marketer chooses a threshold: a tight look-alike (the top 1% most similar) is more precise but small; a broad one (top 10%) reaches more people with lower average quality. Walled gardens now run look-alikes on their own logged-in data, and clean rooms offer them across partners (AWS Clean Rooms ML, for example, lets one party supply a seed and the other a model, with only the resulting audience leaving the room).

      Activation

      For advertisers, DMP segments were activated by sending the segment's IDs to a DSP, either through cookie syncs (the DSP recognises the user when the DMP's pixel fires) or through server-to-server segment files keyed to IDs both platforms had synced. The DSP then bid on those users wherever they appeared. For publishers, the DMP's segments were attached to their own inventory: as key-values passed to the ad server, as segment IDs in bid requests, or packaged into private marketplace deals. Publishers also used DMPs to personalise content, and for audience extension: finding their audience on other sites to sell more of it than their own inventory allowed.

      Data marketplaces and pricing

      DMPs connected buyers to data marketplaces where third-party providers listed segments. Pricing was typically either a fixed CPM (for example $1 per thousand impressions served to members of the segment) or a percentage of media cost (for example 15% of the media spend on the segment). Providers were paid only when their data was used in a delivered impression, so DMPs and DSPs had to report usage back to them. Publishers could sell their own audience data through the same mechanisms.

      The decline of the DMP

      DMPs depended on four things that all weakened at once. Third-party cookies stopped working in Safari (2017 onward) and Firefox (2019 onward), and even first-party cookies set by scripts were capped at seven days in Safari. Consent requirements under GDPR, CCPA and their successors made it hard to justify collecting cross-site data and selling it on. Data quality problems became visible as buyers tested third-party segments and found them inaccurate. And walled gardens offered better targeting on their own logged-in data, which DMPs could not access.

      2021–2026: the DMP vendors exit

      Salesforce retired its DMP, Audience Studio (formerly Krux), steering clients to its CDP. Adobe has moved Audience Manager customers toward its Real-Time CDP. Oracle exited the advertising business entirely, ending its BlueKai DMP and the rest of Oracle Advertising on 30 September 2024, after a privacy class action it settled for $115 million. Lotame, one of the last independent DMPs, was bought by Publicis in March 2025. On the publisher side, the DMP evolved into the publisher data platform (for example Permutive and Optable), built around first-party and contextual data and clean-room collaboration; Safari 27's network block list, which includes Permutive, shows that even this model faces browser limits.

      11.3 The customer data platform

      The CDP Institute defines a customer data platform as packaged software that creates a persistent, unified customer database accessible to other systems. The key words are persistent (profiles are kept over time, not expired like cookie segments), unified (data from every source is combined per customer) and accessible to other systems (the CDP feeds email, ads, apps, websites, call centres and analytics rather than being a marketing tool in its own right).

      Architecture

      A CDP has five functional parts:

      • Ingestion: web and app SDKs that stream events in real time; server-side event APIs; batch imports from CRM, e-commerce, point-of-sale, support and billing systems; and connections to data warehouses.
      • Identity resolution: the heart of the CDP, described below.
      • Profile store: each unified profile holds identifiers, attributes (name, tier, preferences, consents), an event history, and computed traits: values the CDP calculates continuously, such as "orders in the last 90 days", "favourite category", or "days since last visit".
      • Segmentation and prediction: rule-based audiences plus models that score each customer: propensity to buy, propensity to churn, predicted lifetime value, next best product. In 2025–2026 many CDPs added natural-language segment building, where a marketer describes the audience and a language model writes the rule.
      • Activation and orchestration: sending audiences and events to downstream tools (email and SMS platforms, push notifications, website personalisation, call-centre screens) and to advertising platforms, and in some CDPs orchestrating multi-step customer journeys.

      Identity resolution

      Identity resolution decides which records belong to the same customer. CDPs mostly use deterministic rules: two records are joined if they share a reliable identifier. The rules need a priority order (a customer ID beats an email, which beats a phone number, which beats a device ID, which beats a cookie) and limits: if one cookie appears with fifty different email addresses, it is probably a shared or public computer, and merging on it would collapse fifty customers into one. Good CDPs let administrators set these limits and can split profiles that were merged in error.

      Packaged and composable CDPs

      Traditional packaged CDPs (Twilio Segment, Adobe Real-Time CDP, Salesforce's Data Cloud, Tealium, Treasure Data, Amperity, Bloomreach and others) copy data into their own store and provide the whole pipeline. Since about 2021, composable or warehouse-native CDPs have challenged them. In a composable architecture, the company's cloud data warehouse (Snowflake, Google BigQuery, Databricks) is the system of record; a modelling layer builds customer tables there; and a reverse ETL or activation tool (Hightouch, Fivetran Activations, formerly Census, RudderStack) syncs audiences from the warehouse to marketing and ad platforms. Composable setups avoid duplicating data and suit companies with strong data teams; packaged CDPs offer faster real-time processing and more marketer-friendly tools. Most large vendors now offer both modes, for example "zero-copy" access to warehouse data.

      CDPs and advertising

      CDPs connect to advertising in four ways:

      • Audience uploads: sending hashed emails and phone numbers to walled gardens (Google Customer Match, Meta Custom Audiences, Amazon, TikTok, LinkedIn) and retail media networks, which match them to their logged-in users. Because these platforms match directly against their own accounts, they avoid the cookie losses in the funnel above.
      • Suppression: excluding existing customers or recent purchasers from acquisition campaigns, often the quickest return on a CDP.
      • Seeds for look-alikes: supplying high-value customers as seeds for platform look-alike models.
      • Conversion feeds: sending purchase and lead events to platforms' conversion APIs (chapter 8), which is now the main way ad platforms learn what worked.
      2024–2026: CDP consolidation

      The independent CDP market consolidated quickly. Uniphore bought ActionIQ (December 2024), Contentstack bought Lytics (January 2025), Rokt bought mParticle for $300 million (January 2025), and data-movement company Fivetran bought composable CDP Census (May 2025). The CDP Institute still tracked around 208 vendors in mid-2025. The large marketing clouds (Adobe, Salesforce) and warehouse platforms are absorbing CDP functions, and AI features such as predictive traits and natural-language segmentation are now expected rather than differentiating.

      11.4 Data clean rooms

      A CDP solves fragmentation inside one company. But much of the value in advertising data lies between companies: the advertiser knows who bought; the publisher knows who saw the ad; the retailer knows who bought what in store. Sharing those datasets directly would expose customer lists, breach privacy commitments and hand competitors valuable information. A data clean room is the technology built to resolve that tension.

      Data clean roomA secure environment in which two or more parties combine data for agreed purposes, under technical controls that prevent any party from seeing or extracting the other's record-level data. Only approved, privacy-protected outputs leave.

      Why clean rooms appeared

      The first widely used clean rooms were built by walled gardens to replace data they stopped sharing. Google launched Ads Data Hub in 2017 and, from 2018, stopped including user IDs in the log-level files it exported to advertisers, making ADH the way to analyse Google and YouTube campaigns at user level. Amazon followed with Amazon Marketing Cloud. In both cases the platform keeps the user-level data and lets advertisers run approved queries over it, joined with their own data. As third-party cookies weakened and privacy laws tightened, the same model spread to retailers, broadcasters and publishers, and to cloud and independent vendors.

      Core properties

      The IAB Tech Lab's 2023 guidance describes what a clean room must provide. In plain terms:

      • Data stays under its owner's control. Either it never leaves the owner's environment (computation travels to the data) or it is placed in an environment no participant can read directly.
      • Agreed purposes and permissions. Each party configures which of its tables and columns may be used, by whom, for which kinds of analysis.
      • Query controls. Only approved query types or templates can run; dangerous operations (selecting raw rows, filtering to individuals) are blocked.
      • Output controls. Results are aggregated, small groups are suppressed, and noise may be added.
      • Auditability. Every query and output is logged so parties can verify how their data was used.

      Roles

      A clean room collaboration has several roles, which one company may combine: data contributors supply tables; the analyst (or query runner) writes or selects queries; the result receiver gets the output; and the operator runs the environment. In a walled-garden clean room the platform is operator and one contributor, and dictates the rules. In a neutral or cloud clean room, the parties negotiate the rules between them.

      How matching works

      To combine datasets, the parties need a common join key: an identifier both hold for the same person. The most common are normalised and hashed emails and phone numbers; others include mobile advertising IDs, universal IDs such as RampID or UID2, a publisher's logged-in user ID mapped through an identity provider, or household identifiers in TV. The share of one party's records that find a partner in the other's is the match rate, and it limits everything downstream. Identity services inside clean rooms raise match rates by preparing and linking records: AWS, for example, integrates its Entity Resolution service into AWS Clean Rooms, and since September 2025 supports incremental ID mapping so tables need not be fully reprocessed.

      The join itself can be done with different levels of protection. In the simplest case both sides hash identifiers the same way and the clean room joins on the hashes. Stronger designs use private set intersection (PSI): cryptographic protocols in which each party encrypts its identifiers with its own secret key, the parties exchange and re-encrypt each other's values, and matches are found on the doubly encrypted results, so neither learns anything about the other's non-matching records.

      Privacy controls

      Joining data privately is not enough; the outputs must not reveal individuals either. Clean rooms use several layers of control:

      • Aggregation thresholds: any result group with fewer than k people is suppressed. Google's Ads Data Hub, for example, requires roughly 50 users per row by default, with lower thresholds for some click and conversion queries or when noise is applied.
      • Analysis rules and templates: only approved query shapes may run. AWS Clean Rooms, for example, distinguishes aggregation rules (only aggregate outputs over allowed columns), list rules (output the overlapping identifiers for activation) and custom rules (pre-approved SQL).
      • Column and join restrictions: sensitive columns can be used for joining or filtering but never output; some columns can never be joined.
      • Differencing protections: without controls, an analyst could run two queries that differ by one person and subtract the results to learn about that person. Clean rooms block this with query restrictions, overlapping-query checks, and noise.
      • Differential privacy: calibrated random noise is added to outputs so that the presence or absence of any one person changes results only negligibly. A privacy budget (epsilon) limits how much can be learned across many queries.

      Clean rooms differ greatly in how much they rely on cryptography versus contracts and access controls. The IAB Tech Lab's standards encourage stronger privacy-enhancing technologies (PETs). The main ones:

      Three kinds of clean room

      • Walled-garden and media-owner clean rooms: Google Ads Data Hub, Amazon Marketing Cloud, Meta's Advanced Analytics, and clean rooms run by broadcasters, streamers and retailers (Disney reported 140 demand customers using its clean room by January 2024; NBCUniversal, Walmart, Instacart and many others run their own). The platform sets the rules, its data never leaves, and outputs are typically limited to aggregates and audiences usable only on its own media.
      • Cloud-native clean rooms: AWS Clean Rooms, Snowflake's clean rooms (built on its December 2023 acquisition of Samooha), Google BigQuery's data clean rooms and Databricks Clean Rooms. Data stays in each party's cloud account; the cloud provides the controls. Amazon Marketing Cloud itself became available on AWS Clean Rooms, letting advertisers collaborate with Amazon Ads signals without moving their data out of their own AWS environment.
      • Independent collaboration platforms: LiveRamp (which bought Habu for $200 million in January 2024), InfoSum (bought by WPP in April 2025), Optable, Decentriq and others. They work across clouds and partners, often bundle identity services and data marketplaces, and position themselves as neutral. Several are no longer independent of agencies (see below).

      What clean rooms are used for

      • Audience insight and planning: how many of my customers watch this streamer, read this publisher, or shop at this retailer, and how do they differ from the general audience?
      • Activation: creating a matched audience (customers to target, or to exclude) that the media owner can reach on its inventory, without the advertiser learning which of its customers matched. Outputs often go straight into a DSP or the media owner's own platform.
      • Reach and frequency: de-duplicating exposure across publishers, which no single publisher can do alone, to count unique reach and control frequency.
      • Attribution and closed-loop measurement: joining ad exposure logs with the advertiser's sales or the retailer's transaction data to measure conversions, including offline sales.
      • Incrementality: comparing outcomes of exposed and held-out control groups within the matched population to measure lift (chapter 12).
      • Enrichment and modelling: look-alike expansion and joint model training, increasingly with synthetic data.
      • Retail media: the single biggest driver of adoption, because brands want proof that ads on a retailer's network sold products. Even so, fewer than half (48%) of US retail media networks offered clean room capabilities in Q2 2025, according to Mars United Commerce.

      Interoperability standards

      Every clean room had its own matching method and outputs, so a brand working with ten media owners needed ten integrations. The IAB Tech Lab has published a family of standards to fix that:

      • Data Clean Room Guidance & Recommended Practices (2023): baseline privacy, security and governance expectations, roles and operations.
      • Open Private Join & Activation (OPJA) (v1.0, 2023): a protocol for matching advertiser and publisher audiences across clean rooms and activating the result, using encrypted identifiers.
      • PAIR (Publisher Advertiser Identity Reconciliation): originally Google's protocol, merged with OPJA under the Tech Lab in 2024; PAIR 1.1 was finalised in July 2025. In PAIR-style activation, each side encrypts its hashed emails with its own key, the encrypted sets are exchanged and re-encrypted, and matches are found on the doubly encrypted values. The publisher can then label matched users in bid requests with an advertiser-specific identifier the advertiser's DSP recognises, without either side learning the other's non-matching customers, and without a cookie.
      • ADMaP 1.0 (Attribution Data Matching Protocol, February 2025): a standard way to match exposure and conversion data across parties for measurement.

      Limits and criticisms

      • Skills and cost: many clean rooms still require SQL and data-engineering effort, plus fees from the operator and identity providers. The 2025 State of Retail Media report found 39% of organisations struggled to turn clean room data into actionable insight. Amazon removed one barrier in September 2025 by making AMC available at no cost to all Sponsored Ads advertisers.
      • Match rates: results depend on overlap in identifiers; weak overlap means small, biased samples.
      • Fragmentation: a large brand may need Ads Data Hub for Google, AMC for Amazon, Meta's tools for Meta, and separate clean rooms for each retailer and broadcaster, each with different rules, thresholds and outputs.
      • Asymmetric control: in walled-garden clean rooms the platform marks its own homework: it decides what may be measured and how.
      • Neutrality: independent operators are increasingly owned by agencies or identity businesses (see below), raising questions about who sees whose data patterns and whose identifiers are favoured.
      • Not a compliance shortcut: the US Federal Trade Commission warned in late 2024 that using a clean room does not by itself make data sharing lawful. Under GDPR, hashed and encrypted identifiers remain personal data, parties may be joint controllers, and the original collection must have covered this use. Under India's DPDP Act, consent must cover the purpose of the collaboration. Clean rooms operate server to server, so browser restrictions such as Safari's do not stop them directly, but the data going in still needs a lawful basis.
      2023–2026: from "clean room" to "data collaboration"

      The category consolidated fast. Snowflake bought Samooha (December 2023); LiveRamp bought Habu (January 2024); WPP bought InfoSum (April 2025). In May 2026 Publicis agreed to acquire LiveRamp for about $2.2 billion in enterprise value, to be combined with Epsilon's identity assets and run as a neutral platform; the deal is expected to close by the end of 2026, subject to regulatory and shareholder approval. pending Two of the three largest holding companies now own a leading clean room, and Omnicom's acquisition of IPG brought it Acxiom. At the same time the term itself is fading: trade press and vendors increasingly describe clean rooms as one function within broader "data collaboration" platforms, embedded in warehouses, retail media networks and buying platforms rather than bought as a separate product. The IAB reports that more than 80% of clean room users also use CDPs and DMPs: the technologies complement each other.

      Choosing a clean room: questions to ask

      1. Where does my data physically sit during collaboration, and who (including the operator and cloud host) can read it?
      2. Which PETs are used for matching and computation: hashing only, PSI, MPC, TEEs, differential privacy?
      3. What are the default aggregation thresholds, analysis rules and protections against differencing?
      4. Which identifiers can be used as join keys, and is the clean room tied to one identity graph?
      5. Does it support IAB Tech Lab standards (PAIR, ADMaP) for interoperability with partners on other clean rooms?
      6. What outputs are allowed: aggregates only, audiences for one media owner, or audiences portable to any DSP?
      7. Who owns derived data and models, and can the operator use insights across clients?
      8. How are consent, purpose and data-subject requests (deletion, opt-out) enforced inside the room?
      9. What skills are needed to use it day to day: SQL, templates, natural-language interfaces?
      10. What does it cost, including identity resolution, compute and per-partner fees?

      Attribution and measurement

      Assigning credit for results, from last-click rules to marketing mix models and incrementality tests

      Attribution answers a deceptively simple question: which marketing caused this result? It decides where budgets go, so the method chosen shapes the whole market.

      Where visits come from

      Web analytics tools classify each visit by its source, using the referring URL and campaign parameters:

      • Direct: no referrer, because the user typed the URL or used a bookmark, but also when referrers are stripped by apps, secure-to-insecure links or privacy features. "Direct" is often a catch-all for unknown sources.
      • Organic search: unpaid results from search engines.
      • Social: links from social platforms.
      • Referral: links from other websites.
      • Campaign: paid media and email, identified by UTM parameters and click IDs.

      Rule-based attribution models

      A customer's path to purchase usually includes several touchpoints. A rule-based model decides how to share credit among them:

      • Last click gives all credit to the final touchpoint. Simple, but over-rewards channels that capture existing demand, like branded search.
      • Last non-direct click ignores direct visits and credits the last marketing touchpoint before them.
      • First click credits the touchpoint that started the journey, favouring awareness channels.
      • Linear splits credit equally.
      • Time decay gives more credit to touchpoints closer to the conversion, typically halving the weight for every seven days earlier.
      • Position-based (U-shaped) gives 40% each to the first and last touches and divides 20% among the middle.
      • Custom or data-driven models assign weights statistically from observed paths.

      All rule-based models share a weakness: they assume the touchpoints caused the conversion. Many people who click a retargeting ad would have bought anyway. Rules distribute credit; they do not measure causation.

      Cross-device and offline attribution

      People research on phones and buy on laptops, or see an ad online and buy in a shop. Cross-device attribution links touchpoints across devices with deterministic matching (logins) or probabilistic matching. Offline-to-online methods connect TV, radio, print and outdoor to web results through vanity URLs (brand.example/tv), promo codes, time-window analysis (traffic spikes after a TV spot), surveys ("How did you hear about us?") and postal-code matching. Online-to-offline methods connect digital ads to store sales through loyalty cards, matched point-of-sale data, location visits and, in retail media, the retailer's own closed loop.

      Mobile app attribution

      App installs are attributed by mobile measurement partners (MMPs) such as AppsFlyer, Adjust, Branch, Singular and Kochava, which receive clicks and installs and match them, historically using device IDs. On iOS, Apple's SKAdNetwork provides privacy-preserving install attribution without user-level data: delayed, aggregated postbacks with limited conversion values. SKAdNetwork 4 (2022) added multiple postbacks and coarse values; AdAttributionKit (announced June 2024) succeeded it and also works across alternative app marketplaces in the EU.

      Marketing mix modelling and incrementality

      As user-level tracking eroded, two older, aggregate methods returned to the centre:

      • Marketing mix modelling (MMM) uses statistical models of historical spend, sales and outside factors (seasonality, pricing, competition) to estimate each channel's contribution, without any user-level data. Meta's open-source Robyn and Google's open-source Meridian (generally available January 2025) made modern Bayesian MMM accessible beyond large consultancies.
      • Incrementality testing measures causation directly with experiments: show ads to a test group and withhold them from a control group (by user, geography or time), then compare outcomes. The difference is the lift the ads caused. Platforms run "conversion lift" and "geo lift" studies; tests are increasingly used to calibrate MMMs.
      2024–2026: the browser attribution APIs fail

      Google's Attribution Reporting API, the browser-native successor to cookie-based conversion tracking, was retired in October 2025. Mozilla's Privacy-Preserving Attribution (prototyped in Firefox 128 in 2024) and related W3C work continue without broad adoption. Outside walled gardens and Chrome cookies, MMM plus incrementality tests is now the default way to judge what works.

      Key takeaways

      • Rule-based models distribute credit; they do not prove causation.
      • MMPs and SKAdNetwork/AdAttributionKit attribute app installs.
      • MMM (Robyn, Meridian) and incrementality experiments are the default answer to signal loss.

      Ad fraud, waste, brand safety and viewability

      How money leaks out of the supply chain and the standards built to stop it

      Programmatic advertising moves billions of dollars through automated systems that rarely see a human. That attracts fraud, rewards low-quality sites built to harvest ad money, and makes it hard to know whether ads were seen at all.

      Why fraud persists

      Ad fraud is hard to size because the best fraud is never detected and companies that find it rarely publicise it. It persists for structural reasons: money flows automatically to whoever produces impressions and clicks; enforcement is weak compared with financial services; the supply chain is long and fragmented, so each party can blame another; and fraudsters move quickly to whatever channel is growing, from desktop to mobile apps to CTV.

      Common fraud techniques

      • Hidden and stacked ads: ads loaded in 1×1 pixel frames, off-screen, or stacked on top of each other so many impressions are counted for one slot.
      • Domain spoofing: low-quality inventory falsely labelled in bid requests as coming from premium sites, so buyers pay premium prices.
      • Ad injection: browser extensions or malware inserting ads into pages, or replacing a publisher's ads with their own.
      • Click injection and click spamming: in mobile apps, malicious apps fake clicks just before another app's install completes to steal attribution credit.
      • Cookie stuffing: dropping affiliate cookies on users who never clicked, to claim commission on later purchases.
      • Bot traffic: automated browsers and botnets generating impressions and clicks. Methbot (2016) used servers in data centres to fake video views on spoofed premium domains; 3ve (taken down in 2018) used infected home computers. Both stole millions of dollars a day at their peak.
      • CTV and app fraud: server-side ad insertion spoofing, fake devices and spoofed app bundles. CTV's high CPMs make it a prime target.

      Industry defences

      Buyers use fraud detection and verification software that filters general invalid traffic (known bots, data centres) and sophisticated invalid traffic (harder-to-detect bots and manipulation). The IAB Tech Lab created a set of transparency standards:

      • ads.txt (Authorized Digital Sellers, 2017): a public file on a publisher's domain listing every company and account ID authorised to sell its inventory, marked DIRECT or RESELLER. app-ads.txt does the same for apps, linked from the app store listing. Version 1.1 (2022) added fields such as OWNERDOMAIN and MANAGERDOMAIN.
      • sellers.json (2019): a file published by each SSP and exchange listing the companies behind its seller account IDs.
      • SupplyChain object (schain, 2019): a list in each bid request of every intermediary that handled it, so buyers can check each hop against ads.txt and sellers.json.
      • ads.cert: cryptographic signing of bid requests so buyers can verify they were not altered. Its later version, Authenticated Connections, never reached broad adoption.

      Waste: made-for-advertising sites

      Not all wasted spend is fraud. Made-for-advertising (MFA) sites are real websites with real (often bought) traffic, built mainly to show as many ads as possible: dense ad layouts, auto-refreshing slots, thin content and traffic arbitrage. They pass fraud filters and score well on cheap metrics such as viewability and click-through rate, so optimisation algorithms reward them.

      2023: the ANA transparency study

      The Association of National Advertisers audited programmatic buying across many advertisers. Its June 2023 first look (21 advertisers, $123 million, 35 billion impressions) found MFA sites took 15% of spend and 21% of impressions; the average campaign ran on 44,000 websites. Its December 2023 report estimated that about $22 billion of $88 billion in open-web programmatic spend was wasteful or unproductive, and that only about 36 cents of each dollar entering a DSP reached consumers as an effective impression. Buyers responded with MFA exclusion lists and supply-path optimisation; a 2024 ANA benchmark put MFA at about 4% of spend.

      Brand safety and suitability

      Brand safety means keeping ads away from content almost every advertiser avoids (illegal content, hate speech, graphic violence); brand suitability means matching ads to each brand's own tolerance. Tools include keyword and category block-lists, page-level classification, and allow-lists. Blunt keyword blocking has been criticised for defunding news. Research firm Adalytics published reports from 2023 to 2025 alleging ads served on children's sites, to bots declared as human traffic, and YouTube ads on low-quality third-party sites, which shook confidence in verification claims.

      Industry coordination also became legally risky. The Global Alliance for Responsible Media (GARM), a World Federation of Advertisers initiative that set shared brand-safety definitions, shut down in August 2024 days after X sued the WFA and several advertisers, alleging an illegal boycott. When the FTC approved Omnicom's takeover of IPG in 2025, it attached conditions restricting coordinated ad boycotts on political grounds.

      Viewability

      An ad can be served and never seen: below the fold, in a background tab, or scrolled past in a fraction of a second. The MRC and IAB define a viewable impression as at least 50% of the ad's pixels in view for at least one continuous second for display, or two continuous seconds for video (large display ads over 242,500 pixels need only 30% in view). Viewability is measured by JavaScript in the ad or the Open Measurement SDK in apps. Many buyers now go further and buy on attention metrics that combine time in view, screen share and interaction; the MRC has issued guidance on attention measurement.

      Key takeaways

      • Fraud thrives on automation, fragmentation and weak enforcement; it migrates to growing channels like CTV.
      • ads.txt, sellers.json and schain let buyers verify who is selling; ads.cert did not take off.
      • MFA sites were the biggest source of waste exposed in 2023; brand-safety coordination now carries legal risk.

      Privacy, regulation and consent

      The concepts, laws, enforcement, consent signals and platform rules that now govern advertising data, in Europe, the US, India and beyond, and how to build ad tech that respects them

      For most of its history, ad tech collected data first and asked questions later. Since 2018, privacy law, regulatory enforcement, browser engineering, platform policy and court rulings have steadily narrowed what can be collected, shared and used without permission. By 2026 privacy is no longer a compliance appendix to advertising; it shapes the architecture of every system in this book.

      14.1 Why privacy14.2 Core concepts14.3 EU and UK14.4 United States14.5 India14.6 Rest of world14.7 Signals in the bid stream14.8 Browsers and platforms14.9 Ad blocking14.10 Building compliant ad tech

      14.1 Why privacy became the central issue

      Three features of programmatic advertising made it a privacy problem. First, cross-site tracking: third-party cookies, SDKs and identity graphs let companies build detailed behavioural profiles of people who had never heard of them. Second, the bid stream: every real-time auction sends information about the user (identifiers, location, the page being read) to many potential bidders, most of which do not win. Privacy campaigners, notably the Irish Council for Civil Liberties in complaints filed from 2018 onward, argued that RTB is effectively a broadcast of personal data to hundreds of companies with no control over what happens next. Third, data brokers: an industry that buys and resells data, including precise location histories that can reveal visits to clinics, places of worship or homes.

      Public reaction to data scandals, research showing how easily "anonymous" data can be re-identified, and the realisation that location data from ad SDKs was being sold to all comers turned these concerns into law and enforcement. Regulators now treat advertising as one of the main sources of privacy harm online.

      14.2 Core concepts

      Personal data, PII and pseudonymous data

      US practice traditionally focused on personally identifiable information (PII): data that directly identifies someone, such as a name, email address, phone number or government ID. European law uses the broader concept of personal data: any information relating to an identified or identifiable person, directly or indirectly. That includes online identifiers. Newer US state laws and India's DPDP Act have moved toward the broader definition.

      • Pseudonymous data has had direct identifiers replaced (a hashed email, a cookie ID) but can still be linked back to a person with additional information. It remains personal data under GDPR.
      • Anonymous data cannot reasonably be linked to a person by anyone. It falls outside most privacy laws, but genuine anonymisation is hard: combinations of a few attributes (postcode, birth date, gender) can single people out.
      • Sensitive data (GDPR's "special categories"; "sensitive personal information" in US laws) covers health, sex life and orientation, religion, political views, ethnicity, biometrics, and in many US laws precise geolocation and children's data. It needs explicit opt-in consent or is banned for advertising entirely.

      Who is responsible: controllers, processors and joint controllers

      A controller decides why and how personal data is processed; a processor processes it on a controller's documented instructions. Where two companies jointly decide the purposes and means, they are joint controllers and share responsibility. Ad tech blurs these roles: a publisher that embeds a DSP's pixel may be a joint controller for the collection, while the DSP is a separate controller for building its own profiles. US state laws use similar concepts ("controller" and "processor", or in California "business" and "service provider"). India's DPDP Act calls them data fiduciaries and data processors, and the individual a data principal.

      Legal bases, principles and rights

      Under GDPR, every processing operation needs one of six legal bases. For advertising the relevant ones are consent (freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous, and as easy to withdraw as to give) and legitimate interests (a balancing test that regulators have largely rejected for tracking-based advertising). Meta tried relying on contract (personalised ads as part of the service), and Ireland's regulator fined it €390 million in January 2023 for doing so.

      All modern laws share a set of principles: lawfulness and transparency; purpose limitation (data collected for one purpose cannot be reused for an incompatible one); data minimisation (collect only what is needed); accuracy; storage limitation (delete when no longer needed); security; and accountability (be able to prove compliance). High-risk processing, which usually includes large-scale profiling for advertising, requires a data protection impact assessment (DPIA). People have rights to access, correct, delete and port their data, and to object to or opt out of processing for advertising.

      Opt-in versus opt-out

      The single most important difference between regimes is the default. Europe and India are opt-in: tracking for advertising generally needs prior consent. Most US states are opt-out: targeted advertising is allowed until the person objects, except for sensitive data and minors, which need opt-in. California's law frames this as the right to opt out of the "sale" or "sharing" of personal information, where sharing explicitly means disclosure for cross-context behavioural advertising, even if no money changes hands.

      14.3 The European Union and the United Kingdom

      GDPR and ePrivacy

      Two EU laws govern advertising data. The General Data Protection Regulation (applying since 25 May 2018) governs any processing of personal data of people in the EU, with fines up to 4% of global annual turnover. The older ePrivacy Directive, Article 5(3), separately requires consent before storing or reading information on a user's device (cookies, local storage, device fingerprinting), unless strictly necessary for a service the user requested. ePrivacy is enforced by national authorities, which is why France's CNIL can fine companies over cookies directly. Together they produced the consent banners that now cover European websites.

      Regulators have concentrated on how consent is collected. Banners must make refusing as easy as accepting; pre-ticked boxes, "accept" buttons with no equivalent "reject", and continuing to set cookies after refusal are all unlawful. "Cookie walls" that block access unless users accept tracking are not banned outright but must leave a genuinely free choice.

      September 2025: the biggest cookie fines yet

      On 1 September 2025 (announced 3 September), the CNIL fined Google €325 million for showing ads between emails in Gmail's Promotions and Social tabs without consent and for invalid consent to advertising cookies during Google account creation, ordering changes within six months or €100,000 a day. The same day it fined Shein €150 million for setting advertising cookies before users chose, continuing after "reject all", and incomplete information. Google had already been fined €100 million (2020) and €150 million (2021) by the CNIL over cookies. Both companies said they would appeal.

      The Transparency & Consent Framework on trial

      IAB Europe's Transparency & Consent Framework (TCF) is the industry's standard way to collect and pass consent: a registered consent management platform (CMP) shows the banner, records the user's choices for standard purposes and for each listed vendor, and encodes them in a TC String passed to vendors in bid requests. Its legal saga illustrates how courts view ad tech:

      • February 2022: the Belgian data protection authority found TCF v2.0 non-compliant, held IAB Europe to be a joint controller, and fined it €250,000.
      • May 2023: TCF v2.2 removed legitimate interest as a basis for advertising and personalisation purposes and tightened how purposes are described.
      • March 2024: the EU Court of Justice ruled that a TC String can be personal data and that a standard-setting body can be a joint controller.
      • 14 May 2025: the Belgian Market Court upheld the €250,000 fine and the core findings, but limited IAB Europe's joint controllership to the processing of TC Strings, not the advertising processing that follows in OpenRTB. Each side claimed vindication; the ruling did not declare the TCF unlawful.
      • 7 January 2026: the Market Court annulled the DPA's validation of IAB Europe's action plan and ordered it to reassess with the narrower scope.

      "Consent or pay"

      Some publishers and platforms offer a choice between accepting tracking and paying for access. The European Data Protection Board said in an April 2024 opinion that large platforms should generally also offer a free option with less or no behavioural advertising. Meta's case shows how this plays out:

      • November 2023: Meta launched a pay-or-consent model for Facebook and Instagram in the EU.
      • November 2024: after preliminary DMA findings, it added a free "less personalised ads" option based mainly on context.
      • 23 April 2025: the Commission fined Meta €200 million under the DMA for the original binary choice.
      • December 2025: the Commission called a revised choice between fully and less personalised ads a step forward; Meta began offering it in January 2026. Consumer groups say it still falls short. Commission monitoring

      Separately, in November 2025 a Madrid court ordered Meta to pay roughly €480 million to Spanish digital media companies, finding that its GDPR-violating ad practices had given it an unfair advantage. Meta said it would appeal.

      DSA, DMA and data transfers

      The Digital Services Act has, since February 2024, banned online platforms from showing ads based on profiling with sensitive data, or based on profiling at all when the platform is reasonably certain the user is a minor, and requires ad repositories on very large platforms. The Digital Markets Act requires designated gatekeepers to obtain consent before combining personal data across their services or with third-party data for ads. For international data flows, the EU–US Data Privacy Framework (July 2023) restored a legal route for transfers to certified US companies after the earlier Privacy Shield was struck down; the EU General Court dismissed a challenge to it in September 2025, though further appeals are possible.

      The Digital Omnibus

      The United Kingdom

      The UK kept GDPR as "UK GDPR" and its cookie rules in the Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations (PECR). The Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 (Royal Assent 19 June 2025; main provisions in force from 5 February 2026) relaxes cookie consent for low-risk uses: first-party analytics used solely to improve a service, functionality, security and similar cookies no longer need prior consent, provided users get clear information and an opt-out. Advertising cookies and cross-site tracking still require consent, and an analytics setup that also feeds advertising does not qualify. The Act raised cookie fines to UK GDPR levels (from a previous £500,000 cap) and made companies that "instigate" cookies on others' sites directly responsible. The Information Commissioner's Office was replaced by an Information Commission on 30 September 2026.

      14.4 The United States

      The US has no comprehensive federal privacy law; the proposed American Privacy Rights Act (2024) stalled. federal law Regulation comes from states, the Federal Trade Commission, sector laws and litigation.

      State comprehensive privacy laws

      California's CCPA (in force January 2020, strengthened by the CPRA from 2023) was the first. By mid-2026 about 20 states had laws in force and 23 had enacted one (Vermont was 23rd, in June 2026, according to the IAPP; counts vary by tracker). Most follow a common template, usually traced to Virginia and Colorado:

      • Rights to access, correct, delete and port data.
      • Rights to opt out of targeted advertising, the sale of personal data, and significant profiling.
      • Opt-in consent for sensitive data, including precise geolocation in many states.
      • Stricter rules for minors: several states require opt-in consent, or ban targeted advertising, for teenagers under 16 or 18.
      • Data protection assessments for targeted advertising and sale of data.
      • Enforcement by the state attorney general (California also has a dedicated agency), usually with no private right of action.

      A growing number of states require businesses to honour universal opt-out mechanisms, browser or device signals such as Global Privacy Control (GPC), as a valid opt-out of targeted advertising and sale. As of 2026, twelve states require it: California, Colorado, Connecticut, Delaware, Maryland, Minnesota, Montana, Nebraska, New Hampshire, New Jersey, Oregon and Texas. Maryland's law goes further than the template by banning the sale of sensitive data and limiting collection to what is strictly necessary.

      California enforcement

      California has the most active enforcement. The Attorney General settled with Sephora ($1.2 million, 2022) for ignoring GPC signals and with Healthline ($1.55 million, July 2025), including for sharing data for advertising beyond the purpose users expected. The California Privacy Protection Agency, now branded CalPrivacy, fined Honda ($632,500) and Todd Snyder ($345,178) in 2025 for opt-out processes that demanded unnecessary information (Todd Snyder required a selfie with a government ID to process a "Do Not Sell" request) and misconfigured third-party tools, and later fined Ford and others. Under the Delete Act, data brokers must register annually, and from 2026 the Delete Request and Opt-out Platform (DROP) lets Californians send one deletion request to every registered broker; brokers had to begin processing DROP requests on 1 August 2026. CalPrivacy's data broker strike force has fined a string of unregistered brokers, including a marketing firm that sold "custom audiences".

      The FTC and federal law

      The FTC enforces against "unfair or deceptive" practices. In 2024 it banned data brokers X-Mode/Outlogic and InMarket from selling sensitive location data, ordered Avast to pay $16.5 million and stop selling browsing data, and in December 2024 proposed orders against Gravy Analytics and Mobilewalla over location data. The amended COPPA Rule (Children's Online Privacy Protection Act), finalised in January 2025 and fully enforceable from 22 April 2026, requires separate verifiable parental consent before disclosing children's (under-13) data to third parties for targeted advertising, adds biometrics to personal information, and requires written data retention and security programmes.

      States also litigate directly: Texas secured a $1.375 billion settlement from Google in May 2025 over location, incognito browsing and biometric data. Washington's My Health My Data Act (2024) requires consent for consumer health data, including inferences such as purchases of health products, and gives individuals a right to sue. National security laws now restrict data flows too: the 2024 Protecting Americans' Data from Foreign Adversaries Act bars data brokers from selling sensitive data to entities controlled by countries such as China, and a Justice Department rule in force from April 2025 restricts bulk transfers of sensitive personal data to "countries of concern".

      Finally, private litigation under old wiretap and video privacy laws (California's Invasion of Privacy Act, the federal Video Privacy Protection Act) has produced thousands of class actions over tracking pixels and session replay tools on websites, making tag governance a legal issue as well as a technical one.

      14.5 India: the Digital Personal Data Protection Act

      India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP Act) became operational when the DPDP Rules were notified on 13–14 November 2025, after a consultation that drew 6,915 submissions. It applies to digital personal data processed in India, and to processing outside India in connection with offering goods or services to people in India.

      Key obligations

      • Consent-centric processing: data fiduciaries need consent that is free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous, given by a clear affirmative action, and limited to the data necessary for the stated purpose. Withdrawal must be as easy as giving consent. A limited set of "legitimate uses" (such as data voluntarily provided for a specific purpose, or legal obligations) does not need consent; advertising profiling generally does.
      • Notice: each consent request must come with a standalone, plain-language notice listing the data and purposes, and be available in English or any of the 22 languages in the Eighth Schedule of the Constitution.
      • Consent Managers: registered, India-incorporated intermediaries (minimum net worth ₹2 crore) through which people can give, manage, review and withdraw consent across data fiduciaries. Registration opens on 13 November 2026.
      • Children: anyone under 18 is a child. Processing a child's data requires verifiable parental consent, and data fiduciaries must not track, behaviourally monitor or target advertising at children, subject to narrow exemptions notified by the government.
      • Significant Data Fiduciaries: companies designated by the government on the basis of data volume, sensitivity and risk must appoint an India-based Data Protection Officer, run annual DPIAs and audits, and may face data localisation for specified categories.
      • Security and breaches: reasonable safeguards are mandatory; breaches must be reported to affected individuals without delay and to the Data Protection Board, with a detailed report within 72 hours.
      • Rights: access, correction, erasure, grievance redressal and nomination.
      • Cross-border transfers are allowed except to countries the government restricts by notification.

      • Third-party audience data loses its footing. Companies that build segments without a direct relationship with the people involved will struggle to show valid, purpose-specific consent.

      • Contracts and processors: fiduciaries remain responsible for processors (DSPs, SSPs, measurement vendors) acting on their behalf.

      14.7 Consent and privacy signals in the bid stream

      Laws are only as effective as the signals that carry users' choices to every company that touches their data. Several standards do this job.

      • TCF (EU, UK): the CMP stores choices in a TC String: a compact, base64url-encoded record of the CMP and vendor-list versions, consent and legitimate-interest flags for each standard purpose (for example, Purpose 1: store or access information on a device; 3: create profiles for personalised ads; 4: use profiles to select personalised ads; 7: measure ad performance), special features such as precise geolocation, and per-vendor consents. In OpenRTB it travels in regs.gdpr (1 if GDPR applies) and user.consent.
      • Global Privacy Platform (GPP): IAB Tech Lab's container for many jurisdictions in one string, with a header and sections (for example the US national section and state-specific sections, plus the TCF itself). It travels in regs.gpp, with regs.gpp_sid listing which sections apply. It replaced the older US Privacy String (us_privacy, "1YNN").
      • Global Privacy Control: a browser setting that sends an Sec-GPC: 1 HTTP header and exposes navigator.globalPrivacyControl. Sites must translate it into an opt-out and reflect it in GPP.
      • Platform signals: Apple's ATT status determines whether the IDFA is available (passed in device.lmt and an ATT-status extension field); Google's and Meta's own consent modes pass consent states to their tags.

      Receiving a signal creates obligations. A DSP that receives a request with no consent for personalised advertising must not use or build a profile for that user, though it may still bid contextually where allowed. Vendors also strip or coarsen data they do not need: truncating IP addresses, rounding location, dropping identifiers.

      14.9 Ad blocking

      Ad blockers are browser extensions, browsers (Brave) or network-level tools (DNS filters) that stop ad and tracker requests by matching URLs and page elements against community-maintained filter lists such as EasyList. They protect users from intrusive or malicious ads, reduce tracking and speed up pages, but cost publishers revenue.

      Chrome's move from Manifest V2 to Manifest V3 extensions, completed in 2025, replaced the API that let blockers inspect and cancel every network request with a declarative rule system with rule limits. The original uBlock Origin stopped working in Chrome; blockers continue in MV3 versions with somewhat less flexible filtering, and Firefox kept support for the older model.

      Publishers respond in several ways: detecting blockers (for example by checking whether a "bait" element was hidden) and asking users to allow ads; blocking access or offering a reduced experience; asking for payment or offering an ad-free subscription; re-inserting ads from first-party domains; paying to join "acceptable ads" programmes that whitelist less intrusive formats; or leaning on native and sponsored content that blockers do not remove. In Europe, scripts that detect ad blockers may themselves need consent under ePrivacy rules, because they read information from the device. Users can also opt out of interest-based advertising through industry tools such as the NAI and DAA opt-out pages and the AdChoices icon, though these depend on cookies and are easily lost.

      14.10 Building compliant ad tech

      For engineers and architects, privacy law turns into concrete design requirements. A practical checklist:

      1. Map data flows. Inventory every tag, SDK, server-to-server feed and partner; record what data each receives, for what purpose, under which legal basis, and where it is stored.
      2. Gate collection on consent. Load advertising tags and SDKs only after the relevant consent or, in opt-out regimes, only when no opt-out signal (including GPC) is present. Treat server-side tagging as subject to the same rules: moving a pixel to the server does not remove the need for consent.
      3. Carry purpose with the data. Tag records and events with consent state and allowed purposes, and enforce them in pipelines, warehouses, clean rooms and model training.
      4. Propagate signals. Pass TCF and GPP strings correctly in bid requests and APIs, and honour them when receiving: no profiling or identifier use without permission.
      5. Minimise the bid stream. Send only fields the auction needs; truncate IP addresses; coarsen location; omit identifiers where consent is missing; never include sensitive categories in segment names or URLs.
      6. Handle children separately. Detect child-directed services and age signals; disable personalised ads and third-party sharing for minors under COPPA, the DSA, state laws and DPDP.
      7. Set retention limits. Expire raw logs, match tables and audience files on a schedule tied to purpose; delete on withdrawal or request, including in backups and partner systems where contracts require it.
      8. Automate rights requests. Build workflows for access, deletion and opt-out that reach every system and processor; do not demand unnecessary verification for opt-outs (the error that cost Honda and Todd Snyder).
      9. Manage vendors. Contract processors with data processing agreements, check their certifications and sub-processors, register as a data broker where the law requires it, and monitor what their tags actually do.
      10. Assess and document. Run DPIAs for profiling and new data uses, keep consent records as evidence, and log access for audit.

      Key takeaways

      • Cookie IDs, device IDs, IP addresses and hashed emails are personal data under GDPR and increasingly elsewhere; only genuinely aggregate data escapes.
      • Europe and India are opt-in for tracking-based advertising; most US states are opt-out, with opt-in for sensitive data and minors and twelve states requiring GPC.
      • Enforcement escalated in 2025–2026: €325M and €150M CNIL cookie fines, Meta's DMA fine and new ad choice, CalPrivacy fines and DROP, the COPPA amendments.
      • India's DPDP Act bans targeted advertising to under-18s, requires purpose-specific consent and Consent Managers, and carries penalties up to ₹250 crore; full duties apply from 13 May 2027.
      • Signals (TCF, GPP, GPC, ATT) carry choices through the supply chain; compliant ad tech gates collection, carries purpose with data and minimises the bid stream.

      The business of ad tech: vendors, agencies and build vs buy

      How ad tech companies make money and what's hard about running one, how agencies changed, and when to build your own

      Ad tech is a business of thin slices of very large flows. Understanding the business models explains much of the industry's behaviour, from opaque fees to consolidation.

      Business models

      • Percentage of media (take rate): DSPs and SSPs typically charge a share of the spend or revenue flowing through them. Revenue scales with media volume, so platforms want more volume and higher prices.
      • SaaS licence or fixed fee: ad servers, CDPs and analytics tools often charge per seat, per impression served (a CPM fee) or a flat subscription.
      • Media arbitrage (principal-based buying): buying inventory and reselling it at a margin, as ad networks always did and agencies increasingly do.
      • Data and services fees: data CPMs, verification CPMs, managed-service fees.

      Why running an ad tech platform is hard

      Ad tech platforms are among the most demanding systems in commercial software. A large SSP or DSP handles millions of requests per second, each needing a decision within tens of milliseconds, around the clock and across continents. Every request generates logs, so data volumes reach petabytes per day, and reporting must reconcile spend to the cent. Platforms integrate with hundreds of partners on evolving standards, must apply privacy signals for dozens of jurisdictions on every request, and need machine learning for bidding, pacing and fraud detection. Infrastructure cost per request is a core economic constraint: a platform that spends too much on compute to evaluate an impression cannot make money on it.

      How agencies buy media

      Four broad models have coexisted:

      1. Traditional: the agency plans and buys media directly from publishers and networks, and reports to the brand.
      2. Agency trading desk: the agency runs programmatic buying in-house, on licensed DSPs, sometimes using its own proprietary tools.
      3. In-house programmatic: the brand licenses a DSP and runs buying with its own team, gaining control over data and fees.
      4. In-house ad tech: the brand builds or owns key technology itself, such as an ad server, data platform, or bidding logic.

      Brands often mix models: in-house for search and social, agency for TV and complex programmatic.

      2024–2026: the agency map redrawn

      Omnicom announced its acquisition of Interpublic in December 2024 and closed it on 26 November 2025, after unconditional EU approval and FTC approval with conditions restricting coordinated boycotts on political grounds. It retired the DDB, FCB and MullenLowe brands into BBDO, TBWA and McCann, targets $750 million in annual savings, and saw combined headcount fall from about 128,200 to 120,000 during 2025. WPP renamed GroupM as WPP Media in May 2025 amid client losses and a leadership change. Publicis built a data-led model on Epsilon, Profitero and Lotame, and in May 2026 agreed to buy LiveRamp for about $2.2 billion, a deal expected to close by the end of 2026 (chapter 11). Across holding companies, principal media buying, where the agency buys inventory itself and resells it to clients, has grown, raising transparency questions. Self-serve AI tools such as Performance Max, Advantage+ and ChatGPT's Ads Manager reduce the need for trading desks; Forrester expects 85% of US B2C marketing leaders to review agency contracts in 2026.

      Build vs buy (or rent)

      Companies that need ad tech capability (a publisher wanting its own ad stack, a retailer launching a media network, a brand wanting control over bidding) face a classic choice.

      From the business side, renting (licensing a white-label or SaaS platform) is faster to launch, cheaper up front and transfers maintenance risk to the vendor, but leaves fees, data access and roadmap in someone else's hands. Building costs more and takes longer, but can create a differentiated product, capture margin that would go to vendors, and keep data in-house. Many retail media networks started on rented platforms and later built core pieces as they grew.

      From the technology side, building means taking on the scale, latency, integration and compliance challenges described above, and hiring a team that understands both distributed systems and advertising. A common middle path is to build on open-source components (Prebid Server, open-source MMM libraries such as Meridian and Robyn) and cloud services (managed clean rooms, streaming pipelines), and buy the rest.

      Agent protocols change the calculation again. Because AdCP and IAB's AAMP standardise how agents talk to buying and selling platforms, a brand or agency can build its own planning and negotiation agent on top of rented DSP and SSP pipes. The build decision moves up the stack: from building a DSP to building the decision layer above it.

      Key takeaways

      • Most ad tech earns a percentage of media, which aligns vendors with spend volume rather than efficiency.
      • Running a platform means extreme scale, latency, data volume and compliance demands.
      • The holding companies consolidated to five; principal media and self-serve AI are reshaping agency roles.
      • Build vs rent is increasingly about owning the decision layer, not the pipes.

      The state of ad tech in 2026, and what comes next

      Antitrust outcomes, the open web versus answer engines, in-app and CTV, and practical guidance

      Four years ago, the industry's outlook was dominated by identity: how to survive the end of third-party cookies. That question resolved itself differently than expected. The challenges of 2026 are about market power, concentration, platform gatekeepers and AI.

      Antitrust: liable, but intact

      Governments spent 2020–2026 litigating the power of the largest ad platforms. The pattern in the United States is consistent: courts found Google liable but declined to break it up. The most realistic path to a structurally separated Google ad stack now runs through Brussels, where the Commission has argued behavioural remedies are not enough.

      The open web and answer engines

      The open web, meaning independent sites funded by programmatic ads, faces a squeeze from both ends. On the demand side, budgets concentrate in walled gardens and retail media. On the traffic side, AI answer engines resolve questions without sending visitors: Google's AI Overviews (US launch May 2024) and AI Mode answer on the results page, and chat assistants answer in conversation. Publishers call the endpoint "Google Zero". After AI Overviews launched in France in July 2026, publishers led by the press association APIG filed a new competition complaint. WPP Media forecasts generative-search advertising at $5.1 billion in 2026, about 0.4% of the market, passing $100 billion by 2030 (a forecast, not a result).

      In-app mobile

      After ATT, iOS in-app advertising shifted toward contextual signals, SKAdNetwork/AdAttributionKit measurement and platforms with strong first-party data. AppLovin emerged as the category winner: it sold its apps business, grew 2025 revenue 70% to $5.48 billion on its Axon machine-learning engine, and opened a self-serve Axon Ads Manager to e-commerce advertisers by referral in October 2025. Short sellers alleged data misuse in early 2025 and Bloomberg reported an SEC probe in October 2025; no wrongdoing has been charged. SEC On Android, the advertising ID survives after the retirement of Privacy Sandbox on Android. In April 2025 a US court barred Apple from charging commissions on, or restricting, links from apps to external web checkouts, making web purchase flows a viable path for advertiser-funded apps.

      CTV and streaming

      The old CTV problems were fragmentation, identity, measurement and fraud. Fragmentation remains (many apps, operating systems and devices), but the big streamers now sell through standard DSPs, and household-level identity is anchored by logged-in streaming accounts, TV operating systems (Roku, Samsung, LG, Walmart's Vizio, The Trade Desk's Ventura) and partnerships like Amazon–Roku. With supply growing as fast as demand, CPMs have flattened; the competition is now over who controls the operating system and the household graph.

      DOOH and retail media

      Digital out-of-home has recovered from the pandemic collapse and increasingly trades programmatically, with audience estimates from mobility data; measurement standards and per-screen inventory variability remain its challenges. Retail media continues to grow faster than other channels, but its measurement is self-reported by retailers with an interest in the result; the IAB/MRC Retail Media Measurement Guidelines (finalised January 2024) were a first step toward standardisation.

      Practical guidance for 2026

      1. Build for two browser worlds. Chrome still has third-party cookies; Safari has neither cookies nor the named identity vendors. Prioritise authenticated first-party data, server-side measurement and contextual signals.
      2. Treat Prebid as strategic. Court-mandated interoperability makes it the neutral auction layer for the next six years.
      3. Plan for agentic interfaces, but don't re-platform yet. AdCP and IAB AAMP are still competing; both sit on top of OpenRTB.
      4. Measure with MMM and incrementality tests rather than cookie-based multi-touch attribution.
      5. Audit the supply path. Use ads.txt, sellers.json and schain checks, MFA exclusions and direct paths; ask what share of each dollar reaches the publisher.
      6. In India, budget for DPDP now. Decide on consent-manager integration from 13 Nov 2026; substantive obligations, including children's data rules, apply from 13 May 2027.

      What to watch

      • Final judgment and appeals in US v. Google (ad tech), and whether the EU orders a structural remedy.
      • Whether Apple expands or publishes the criteria for Safari's identity block list.
      • Convergence or competition between AdCP and IAB AAMP.
      • How fast ads in AI assistants grow, and how answer engines affect publisher traffic.
      • The European Parliament's position on the Digital Omnibus cookie rules.
      • The Trade Desk's recovery or further decline as a bellwether for the independent open-web stack.

      Key takeaways

      • US courts found Google liable in search and ad tech but imposed behavioural remedies; the EU may go further.
      • The open web is squeezed by walled-garden concentration and AI answer engines.
      • CTV and in-app are now mature, platform-dominated markets; retail media keeps growing.

      Glossary

      Terms used in this book
      AAMP
      Agentic Advertising Management Protocols: IAB Tech Lab's family of standards for AI agents buying and selling media.
      AdCP
      Advertising Context Protocol: an open agent protocol for advertising built on MCP and A2A, governed by AgenticAdvertising.Org.
      Ad exchange
      A marketplace where impressions are auctioned between SSPs and DSPs.
      Ad server
      Software that decides which ad to show, delivers it and counts it. Publisher (first-party) and advertiser (third-party) versions exist.
      ads.txt / app-ads.txt
      Public files listing the companies authorised to sell a site's or app's inventory.
      ATT
      App Tracking Transparency: Apple's opt-in requirement for cross-app tracking and IDFA access.
      Bid shading
      A DSP technique for bidding below value in first-price auctions while still winning.
      Aggregation threshold
      The minimum number of people a clean room result group must contain before it is shown (often written k).
      CDP
      Customer data platform: unifies a company's first-party customer data into persistent profiles accessible to other systems.
      Consent Manager
      Under India's DPDP Act, a registered intermediary through which people give, manage and withdraw consent across services.
      Composable CDP
      A CDP built on the company's own data warehouse, with audiences synced out by reverse ETL.
      Computed trait
      A profile attribute a CDP calculates continuously, such as orders in the last 90 days.
      Clean room
      A controlled environment where parties combine data for matching or measurement without exposing raw records.
      Contextual targeting
      Targeting based on page or app content rather than user identity.
      Cookie syncing
      Mapping two companies' cookie IDs for the same browser through redirects.
      CPM / CPC / CPA
      Cost per thousand impressions, per click, per action.
      CTV / OTT
      Connected TV (internet-connected TV sets) and over-the-top streaming delivery.
      Curation
      Sell-side packaging of inventory and audience data into deals any DSP can buy.
      Data fiduciary / principal
      DPDP Act terms for the controller of personal data and the individual it relates to.
      Data onboarding
      Matching offline records (such as a CRM file) to online identifiers for advertising.
      Delete Act / DROP
      California law requiring data broker registration, with a platform letting residents send one deletion request to all brokers.
      Deal ID
      An identifier attached to bid requests for a negotiated programmatic deal.
      DMP
      Data management platform: collected and segmented cookie-based audience data; now largely absorbed into CDPs and clean rooms.
      Differential privacy
      Adding calibrated noise to outputs so no individual's presence measurably changes results, limited by a privacy budget.
      DOOH
      Digital out-of-home: digital screens in public places.
      DSP
      Demand-side platform: buyer software for bidding across exchanges.
      First-price auction
      Auction in which the winner pays its own bid; the programmatic standard since 2019.
      Floor price
      The minimum price a seller will accept for an impression.
      Frequency cap
      A limit on how often one person sees an ad in a period.
      GPP / GPC
      Global Privacy Platform (IAB consent and opt-out signal string) and Global Privacy Control (browser opt-out signal).
      Header bidding
      Requesting bids from many demand partners in parallel before the ad server decides; usually via Prebid.
      Identity resolution
      Deciding which records belong to the same person, deterministically (shared identifiers) or probabilistically.
      IDFA / GAID
      Apple's and Google's resettable mobile advertising identifiers.
      Incrementality
      The extra outcomes caused by advertising, measured with test and control groups.
      MFA site
      Made-for-advertising site: built mainly to monetise bought traffic with dense ads.
      MMM
      Marketing mix modelling: statistical estimation of each channel's contribution from aggregate data.
      MMP
      Mobile measurement partner: attributes app installs and in-app events.
      Legitimate interests
      A GDPR legal basis requiring a balancing test; regulators have largely rejected it for tracking-based advertising.
      Look-alike model
      A model that expands a seed audience to statistically similar people.
      OpenRTB
      The IAB Tech Lab protocol for bid requests and responses; version 2.6 is current.
      PAIR
      Publisher Advertiser Identity Reconciliation: an IAB Tech Lab standard (originally Google's) for matching advertiser and publisher audiences with double encryption and activating them without cookies.
      Personal data
      Any information relating to an identifiable person, including cookie IDs, device IDs, IP addresses and hashed emails.
      PMP / PG
      Private marketplace (invite-only auction) and programmatic guaranteed (fixed-price, reserved deal).
      Private set intersection (PSI)
      Cryptographic matching that reveals only which records two parties have in common.
      Pseudonymisation
      Replacing direct identifiers so data cannot be linked to a person without extra information; the data remains personal.
      Prebid
      Open-source header bidding software (Prebid.js, Prebid Server, Prebid Mobile).
      Privacy Sandbox
      Google's proposed browser APIs to replace third-party cookies; most ad APIs retired October 2025.
      Reverse ETL
      Syncing modelled data from a warehouse into operational and marketing tools.
      Retail media network
      A retailer's advertising business, using its shopper data on and off its own properties.
      RTB
      Real-time bidding: auctioning each impression individually as it loads.
      SafeFrame
      An IAB iframe standard with an API for ad position, viewability and expansion.
      schain
      OpenRTB SupplyChain object: the list of intermediaries that handled a bid request.
      sellers.json
      A file published by SSPs and exchanges identifying the companies behind seller accounts.
      SKAdNetwork / AdAttributionKit
      Apple's privacy-preserving app install attribution frameworks.
      SSP
      Supply-side platform: publisher software for selling impressions to many buyers.
      SPO
      Supply-path optimisation: reducing the intermediaries between buyer and publisher.
      TCF / TC String
      IAB Europe's Transparency & Consent Framework; choices for standard purposes and vendors are encoded in a TC String passed in user.consent.
      Universal opt-out mechanism
      A browser or device signal, such as GPC, that states must treat as an opt-out of targeted ads and sale (12 US states in 2026).
      UID2 / EUID
      Unified ID 2.0 and European Unified ID: email-based universal identifiers.
      VAST / SIMID / OM SDK
      Video ad serving template; interactive video ad standard; Open Measurement SDK for verification.
      Viewable impression
      MRC standard: 50% of pixels in view for 1 second (display) or 2 seconds (video).
      Walled garden
      A platform that owns a logged-in audience and sells access only through its own tools.
      Waterfall
      Offering an impression to demand sources one at a time in a fixed order.
